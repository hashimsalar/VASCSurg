Peripheral arterial disease and claudication

PAD is atherosclerotic narrowing of the limb arteries. It runs on a spectrum from asymptomatic, through intermittent claudication, to chronic limb-threatening ischemia at the severe end.

Intermittent claudication is reproducible exertional muscle pain, cramping or fatigue that is relieved by a few minutes of standing still. It is a quality-of-life problem with a low rate of limb loss (on the order of 1 to 2 percent a year). The real danger to these patients is their coexisting coronary and cerebrovascular disease, which is why every claudicant is a cardiovascular risk-reduction patient first.

The two historical staging systems are worth knowing because attendings switch between them freely.

Fontaine stages and Rutherford categories (chronic ischemia)

Management is medical first

  • Risk-factor therapy for everyone. Smoking cessation, a high-intensity statin, an antiplatelet, and control of blood pressure and diabetes. Low-dose rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily) with aspirin is an option in selected patients to cut limb and cardiovascular events. Details are in Foundations.
  • Supervised exercise therapy. First-line for walking distance, and it rivals revascularization for claudication. Cilostazol adds a modest benefit if there is no heart failure.
  • Revascularization only for lifestyle-limiting symptoms that persist despite medical therapy and exercise, in a patient who understands the trade-off. The choice between endovascular and open repair depends on the location and length of the disease. Interventions below the knee for claudication alone are generally discouraged, because durability is poor and failure can make the limb worse.
A 64-year-old smoker can walk two blocks before calf pain stops him. What do you offer first?

Smoking cessation, a high-intensity statin, an antiplatelet, and a supervised exercise program, with cilostazol if there is no heart failure. Claudication is treated medically first; intervention is reserved for lifestyle-limiting symptoms that persist after a fair trial.

Chronic limb-threatening ischemia

CLTI, the modern term for "critical limb ischemia," is the severe end of PAD: ischemic rest pain for more than two weeks, a non-healing ulcer, or gangrene, together with objectively reduced perfusion.

Rest pain is classically in the forefoot and toes, worse lying flat at night, and relieved by hanging the foot off the bed, because gravity restores a trickle of perfusion. Many patients sleep in a chair. Ask about it specifically, because they rarely volunteer it.

Pearl: WIfI

The WIfI classification stages limb threat along three axes: Wound, Ischemia (ABI, ankle pressure or toe pressure) and foot Infection, each graded 0 to 3. It reframes the decision from "is there a pulse" to "what is this limb's risk of amputation and how much would revascularization help," which is how vascular surgeons now think. Grades are in Quick reference and how to document them is in charting.

GLASS (the Global Limb Anatomic Staging System) is the anatomic partner to WIfI. It grades the femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal disease along a chosen target artery path, to estimate how likely an endovascular approach is to succeed. Together, WIfI and GLASS frame the patient, the limb and the anatomy, the structure used by the Global Vascular Guidelines.

What the trials changed

  • BEST-CLI (2022). In patients with an adequate single-segment great saphenous vein, a surgical bypass-first strategy led to fewer major adverse limb events or deaths than an endovascular-first strategy. Without good vein, the two strategies performed similarly. This revived vein bypass in the right patient and made vein mapping a routine part of the workup.
  • BASIL-2 (2023). For CLTI needing an infrapopliteal revascularization, a best-endovascular-first strategy gave better amputation-free survival than vein bypass first, driven mainly by fewer deaths. It applies to a different, more distal population than BEST-CLI.

The practical upshot is individual: the patient's risk, the limb's WIfI stage, the anatomy, and the availability of good vein all shape the choice. CLTI demands revascularization wherever feasible, plus aggressive wound care, offloading and infection control.

Red flag

Untreated CLTI carries a high one-year risk of major amputation and death. Rest pain, a non-healing wound, or any gangrene is not something to watch; it needs a revascularization plan.

Resident tip

On every CLTI consult, ask whether the great saphenous veins are still present and order vein mapping early if bypass is possible. "Has the vein been used for a CABG or a prior bypass?" is the question that most often changes the plan.

Acute limb ischemia Emergency

ALI is a sudden loss of arterial perfusion that threatens limb viability, presenting within two weeks of onset. Muscle tolerates roughly 4 to 6 hours of severe ischemia before irreversible injury begins.

Mnemonic: the six Ps

Pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (the perishingly cold limb). The order matters clinically, because paresthesia and paralysis are the late, ominous signs. A limb with sensory or motor loss is on the clock; pain and pallor alone are earlier and more recoverable.

Embolic or thrombotic: the central distinction

The mechanism changes the operation, so this comparison is asked relentlessly.

What is the most common source of an arterial embolus, and where does it lodge in the leg?

The heart, most often from atrial fibrillation. Emboli lodge at branch points where the vessel narrows; in the leg the common femoral bifurcation is the classic site.

Management

  1. Anticoagulate immediately. A systemic heparin bolus and infusion the moment ALI is suspected, unless contraindicated, to stop clot propagation. Do not wait for imaging.
  2. Grade the limb. The Rutherford class decides the path: I and IIa allow time for CTA and possibly catheter-directed therapy; IIb needs revascularization now, usually in the OR; III is not salvageable.
  3. Revascularize. Balloon embolectomy with a Fogarty catheter (classic for embolic disease), catheter-directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy (often suited to thrombotic disease and category I to IIa limbs), surgical bypass, or a hybrid of these.
  4. Anticipate reperfusion. Restoring flow to ischemic muscle releases potassium, myoglobin and acid. Watch for hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury and compartment syndrome, and keep a low threshold for fasciotomy.
  5. Find the source. After an embolus: ECG and telemetry for atrial fibrillation, an echocardiogram, and a plan for long-term anticoagulation.

Red flag: reperfusion injury

After revascularizing a limb that was ischemic for several hours, a tense, painful compartment with pain on passive stretch is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Pulses may still be present, so do not wait for them to disappear. The treatment is urgent four-compartment fasciotomy. Watch the potassium and the kidneys at the same time.

Resident tip: the first ten minutes

Examine and Doppler both legs and write down exactly what you find, including sensation and toe movement. Establish the time of onset and whether there is atrial fibrillation. Start heparin unless there is a clear contraindication, send a CBC, BMP, coags, CK, lactate and type and screen, and call your senior with the Rutherford class. Overnight versions of this call are on On call.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm

An aneurysm is a focal dilation more than 1.5 times the normal vessel diameter. For the infrarenal aorta that means 3.0 cm or more. Most are infrarenal and degenerative.

Risk factors are smoking (the strongest modifiable one), male sex, older age, a family history in a first-degree relative, and other atherosclerotic disease. Diabetes is, curiously, protective.

Pearl: true and false aneurysms

A true aneurysm involves all three layers of the wall: intima, media and adventitia. A pseudoaneurysm, or false aneurysm, is a contained rupture held in only by the outer layer or the surrounding tissue, classically at an anastomosis or a femoral puncture site. The distinction is a frequent question.

Screening and surveillance

The USPSTF recommends a one-time screening ultrasound for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked, with selective screening for men who never smoked. The SVS recommends a broader net: men and women aged 65 to 75 with a smoking history, and people with a first-degree relative with an AAA. Small aneurysms are followed by ultrasound at intervals that shorten as they grow.

When to repair electively

  • Diameter of 5.5 cm or more in men.
  • Diameter of 5.0 cm or more in women (European guidance; the SVS suggests considering repair from 5.0 to 5.4 cm in women).
  • Rapid growth: more than 1 cm in a year, or more than 0.5 cm in six months.
  • Symptoms at any size: tenderness, back or abdominal pain attributable to the aneurysm, or distal embolization.

Repair is by EVAR when the anatomy suits it, or open repair. EVAR has lower early mortality, the long-term survival of the two converges, and EVAR needs lifelong imaging surveillance because of endoleak and late failure. Both are covered in Open operations and Endovascular.

At what size do you repair an asymptomatic infrarenal AAA in a man?

5.5 cm (5.0 cm in women), or sooner for rapid growth or symptoms. Below threshold the rupture risk is lower than the operative risk, so you survey the aneurysm and optimize medical therapy.

Red flag: ruptured AAA

The classic triad is sudden abdominal, back or flank pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile mass. An older patient with hypotension and back pain is a ruptured AAA until proven otherwise. Call the attending, the OR and the blood bank (massive transfusion) at once. Practice permissive hypotension, keeping systolic pressure only high enough to keep the patient awake and perfusing (around 70 to 90 mmHg), because aggressive fluid and a higher pressure can blow out a contained rupture. A stable patient may get a CTA to plan EVAR, which is preferred when the anatomy allows; an unstable one goes straight to the OR, where an aortic occlusion balloon can buy control.

Variants to recognize

Inflammatory AAA
A thick, inflamed wall with a peri-aortic rind, raised inflammatory markers, often pain, and classically ureteral entrapment. The inflammation often settles after repair.
Mycotic (infected) AAA
Saccular, often in an unusual location, rapidly expanding, in a febrile patient. Salmonella and Staphylococcus are classic organisms. Needs prolonged antibiotics and repair, with attention to infected-field reconstruction.

Carotid disease and stroke prevention

Plaque at the carotid bifurcation causes stroke mainly by embolization of plaque or thrombus to the brain, and less often by flow limitation from a tight stenosis. The whole point of carotid intervention is stroke prevention, not symptom relief.

Symptomatic or asymptomatic

Symptomatic disease means an ipsilateral neurologic event in the previous six months: a TIA, a stroke, or amaurosis fugax (transient monocular blindness, "a curtain coming down," from a retinal artery embolus). Asymptomatic disease is found on screening or because of a bruit.

What is amaurosis fugax and what does it signify?

Transient, painless loss of vision in one eye from an embolus to the ophthalmic or retinal artery, a branch of the internal carotid. It is a form of TIA and makes that carotid symptomatic, lowering the threshold to intervene.

Indications, and what the trials showed

NASCET and ECST established the benefit of endarterectomy for symptomatic stenosis. ACAS and ACST-1 showed a smaller benefit in asymptomatic patients, in an era before modern medical therapy.

CREST-2 (reported in 2025) compared intensive medical management alone with revascularization plus intensive medical management in asymptomatic patients with severe stenosis. In the stenting comparison, adding stenting lowered the rate of stroke; in the endarterectomy comparison, the benefit did not reach statistical significance. How this changes practice for asymptomatic disease is still being worked out, so expect your attendings to discuss it and guidelines to move.

Endarterectomy, transfemoral stenting or TCAR

Carotid endarterectomy remains the reference standard. Transfemoral carotid stenting is an alternative, particularly for a hostile neck (prior radiation or neck surgery, a high lesion) or high surgical risk, but CREST showed stenting carried more periprocedural strokes and surgery more myocardial infarctions, with stenting faring worse in older patients. Transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) uses a small incision at the base of the neck, direct carotid access and flow reversal for embolic protection, avoiding the aortic arch, and has become a widely used middle path. The operations are described in Open operations.

Red flag: timing after a stroke

After a stroke or TIA, revascularization is generally done within about two weeks in stable patients, because recurrence risk is front-loaded. It is deferred in the hyperacute setting of a large infarct or hemorrhagic transformation, where restored flow can cause bleeding. Coordinate the timing with neurology.

Mesenteric ischemia

Reduced blood flow to the gut, split into an acute emergency and a chronic, indolent form. The vessels at stake are the celiac trunk, the SMA and the IMA.

Acute mesenteric ischemia Emergency

The hallmark is pain out of proportion to the exam: a patient in severe abdominal pain with a soft, relatively benign-feeling abdomen early on. Peritonitis, bloody stool and shock are late signs that usually mean dead bowel.

The workup shows leukocytosis, a rising lactate and a metabolic acidosis, although a normal lactate early does not exclude it. CTA is the imaging test of choice. Management is to resuscitate, start heparin, give broad-spectrum antibiotics, revascularize urgently (embolectomy, bypass, or endovascular stenting, sometimes retrograde through an open SMA), and resect frankly necrotic bowel, with a planned second-look operation at 24 to 48 hours for marginal segments.

Why does an SMA embolus spare the proximal jejunum?

The embolus typically lodges a few centimeters into the SMA, distal to the first jejunal branches and the middle colic origin, so the proximal jejunum (and sometimes the proximal colon) keeps its perfusion while the rest of the small bowel infarcts. A thrombosis at the origin, by contrast, takes the whole SMA territory.

Chronic mesenteric ischemia

"Intestinal angina." The triad is postprandial abdominal pain, food fear (sitophobia) and weight loss, in an older patient with vascular disease. Because the collateral network is rich, symptoms usually need significant disease in at least two of the three vessels. Treatment is revascularization, now mostly endovascular stenting of the SMA, with open bypass for selected patients.

Pearl: median arcuate ligament syndrome

Extrinsic compression of the celiac artery by the median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm, classically in younger, thinner patients with postprandial pain and an epigastric bruit that changes with respiration. It is a diagnosis of exclusion, and treatment is surgical release of the ligament, not stenting, because a stent is crushed by the ligament.

Aortic dissection Emergency

A tear in the intima lets blood track into the media, creating a false lumen that can propagate, occlude branch vessels (malperfusion), or rupture.

The presentation is sudden, severe, tearing chest or back pain, sometimes with a pulse or blood pressure difference between the arms, or signs of organ malperfusion: a cold leg, abdominal pain with a rising lactate, falling urine output, or a neurologic deficit.

Dissection classifications
StanfordDeBakeyInvolvesDefault management
Type AI and IIThe ascending aorta (I: ascending plus arch and beyond; II: ascending only)Emergency open repair by cardiac surgery; risk of tamponade, aortic insufficiency and coronary involvement
Type BIIIThe descending aorta, distal to the left subclavianMedical first; TEVAR if complicated

The 2020 SVS/STS reporting system classifies by the location of the entry tear: type A if it is in zone 0 (the ascending aorta), type B if it is beyond zone 0, with subscripts for the proximal and distal extent by aortic zone (for example B3,10). A type B tear whose dissection extends back into the arch is written B0 or B1 or B2, the group older papers called "non-A non-B." Chronicity is hyperacute (under 24 hours), acute (1 to 14 days), subacute (15 to 90 days) and chronic (over 90 days). Zones are explained in Endovascular.

Initial medical management

The goal is to reduce the force of each heartbeat on the aortic wall (the rate of rise of pressure) and the pressure itself. Start an IV beta-blocker first (esmolol or labetalol), then add a vasodilator such as nicardipine, clevidipine or nitroprusside if the pressure is still high. The 2022 ACC/AHA targets are a heart rate of 60 to 80 and a systolic under 120 mmHg, or the lowest pressure that keeps the brain, kidneys and gut perfused. Older teaching used a heart rate under 60, so confirm the target your attending wants. Treat the pain, place an arterial line, and admit to the CTV ICU.

Red flag: sequence matters

Never give a vasodilator before beta-blockade. A vasodilator alone triggers reflex tachycardia, which increases the shear on the wall and can extend the dissection. Beta-blocker first, always. If a beta-blocker is contraindicated, a non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem or verapamil) controls the rate.

Type A dissections go to cardiac surgery emergently. Type B dissections are managed medically unless complicated by malperfusion, rupture, rapid expansion, or pain or hypertension that will not settle, in which case TEVAR to cover the entry tear is the usual intervention. Uncomplicated type B dissections with high-risk features (such as a total aortic diameter of 40 mm or more, or a large entry tear) are increasingly considered for TEVAR in the subacute window.

A patient with a type B dissection on an esmolol drip develops a cold, pulseless left leg. What has happened, and what changes?

Limb malperfusion: the false lumen is compressing or the flap is obstructing the iliac flow. The dissection is now complicated, which moves it from medical management toward urgent intervention, usually TEVAR to cover the entry tear and re-expand the true lumen, with or without a direct limb procedure. Call the attending now.

Deep vein thrombosis and venous thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism spans DVT and pulmonary embolism. Its pathophysiology is captured by Virchow's triad.

Mnemonic: Virchow's triad

Stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability. Every DVT risk factor (immobility, surgery, malignancy, estrogen, pregnancy, inherited thrombophilia) maps onto one of the three.

Diagnosis combines pretest probability (the Wells score), D-dimer (useful to rule out disease when probability is low) and compression duplex ultrasound, the confirmatory test, which shows a non-compressible vein.

Treatment

  • Anticoagulation is the treatment. A direct oral anticoagulant is first-line for most patients, including many with cancer. Low-molecular-weight heparin or warfarin suits others (pregnancy, severe renal failure, antiphospholipid syndrome).
  • Duration. About three months for a DVT provoked by a transient risk factor such as surgery. For an unprovoked DVT or a persistent risk factor, extended anticoagulation is considered, balanced against bleeding risk.
  • Isolated calf DVT. Either anticoagulate or repeat the ultrasound in one to two weeks to look for extension, depending on symptoms and risk.
  • Thrombus removal. Catheter-directed lysis or mechanical thrombectomy for selected patients with acute iliofemoral DVT, severe symptoms and low bleeding risk. ATTRACT (2017) did not reduce post-thrombotic syndrome overall but did reduce its severity, with the best signal in iliofemoral disease; CAVA (2020) found no reduction. So it is a selective, not a routine, therapy.
  • IVC filters. The clear indication is acute proximal DVT or PE in a patient who cannot be anticoagulated. Placing a filter for recurrent PE despite anticoagulation is debated, and PREPIC2 found no benefit from adding a filter to anticoagulation. Plan the retrieval when the filter goes in.

Red flag: phlegmasia

Massive iliofemoral DVT can cause phlegmasia cerulea dolens: a swollen, blue, painful leg from near-total venous outflow obstruction that can progress to venous gangrene and compartment syndrome. It is a limb-threatening emergency that needs urgent thrombus removal (lysis or thrombectomy), not anticoagulation alone. Its milder, pale precursor is phlegmasia alba dolens.

Pearl: May-Thurner

May-Thurner syndrome is compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery against the spine, predisposing to left-leg DVT, classically in younger women. It is why an "unprovoked" left iliofemoral DVT deserves a look at the iliac vein, often treated with thrombus removal and venous stenting under IVUS guidance.

Superficial thrombophlebitis of the great saphenous vein extends to within 2 cm of the saphenofemoral junction. How do you treat it?

As a DVT: therapeutic anticoagulation, because thrombus within about 3 cm of the junction can propagate into the common femoral vein. Longer superficial thromboses farther from the junction (5 cm or more in length) are commonly treated with prophylactic-dose fondaparinux for about 45 days.

Chronic venous insufficiency and varicose veins

When venous valves fail, blood refluxes and venous pressure stays high (venous hypertension), producing varicose veins, heaviness, edema, skin changes and ultimately ulceration.

Venous or arterial ulcer?

Venous ulcer

  • Medial malleolus, the gaiter area
  • Irregular, shallow, granulating and exudative
  • Mild pain, better with elevation
  • Pigmentation, edema, lipodermatosclerosis around it
  • Pulses present
  • Cornerstone: compression

Arterial ulcer

  • Toes, heel, pressure points and distal tips
  • Punched out, dry, with a pale or necrotic base
  • Severe pain, worse with elevation, better dependent
  • Shiny, hairless, cool, atrophic skin
  • Pulses diminished or absent
  • Cornerstone: revascularization

Red flag: check the inflow before you compress

Never apply compression to a leg you have not confirmed has adequate arterial inflow, because compressing an ischemic leg can cause tissue loss. Check the ABI before wrapping a "venous-looking" ulcer. As a rough guide, full compression is avoided when the ABI is below about 0.5 to 0.6, and reduced compression is used in the mixed range below 0.8.

Management

Treatment starts with compression and elevation. For refluxing superficial veins, the options are endovenous thermal ablation (radiofrequency or laser), non-thermal ablation (cyanoacrylate glue or mechanochemical ablation), foam sclerotherapy, and phlebectomy for the varicosities themselves. For a venous ulcer, the EVRA trial (2018) showed that early ablation of superficial reflux, added to compression, healed ulcers faster and reduced recurrence. After thermal ablation, a duplex looks for endovenous heat-induced thrombosis (EHIT) extending toward the deep vein.

The diabetic foot

Diabetic foot disease is the convergence of three processes: neuropathy, ischemia and infection. Each worsens the others, and together they make the diabetic foot the leading cause of non-traumatic amputation.

  • Neuropathy. Loss of protective sensation leads to unnoticed trauma. Neuropathic ulcers sit under pressure points (metatarsal heads, heel) in a foot that may have intact circulation.
  • Ischemia. Diabetic PAD favors the tibial vessels below the knee, often sparing the foot arteries, and calcification can make the ABI falsely high, so use toe pressures.
  • Infection. Can spread fast along tendon sheaths and compartments with surprisingly little systemic response. A deep space infection or wet gangrene is a surgical emergency for drainage first.
  • Charcot foot. Progressive neuropathic joint destruction producing the collapsed "rocker-bottom" foot. In the acute phase it can mimic infection (red, hot, swollen) without a wound.

Severity is staged with WIfI, which has largely replaced older systems such as Wagner and the University of Texas classification for limb-threat decisions. A positive probe-to-bone test suggests osteomyelitis; X-ray and then MRI help confirm it.

Pearl

Management is multidisciplinary and follows the same logic as the rest of the service: drain and debride and control infection first, offload pressure, and revascularize if there is ischemia. Because diabetic disease favors the tibial vessels with patent pedal targets, a distal bypass or a pedal intervention can save a foot that looks unsalvageable. Many centers run this as a "toe and flow" team with podiatry.

Other key presentations

Briefer, but each is a known consult and a known question.

Popliteal artery aneurysm clots, not ruptures
The most common peripheral aneurysm. The danger is thrombosis and distal embolization causing limb loss, not rupture. Often bilateral and associated with an AAA, so image the other knee and the aorta. Repair is generally offered at about 2 cm or more, or with mural thrombus or symptoms at any size.
Splenic and other visceral aneurysms pregnancy risk
The splenic artery is the most common visceral aneurysm, with a female predominance and a notable risk of rupture in pregnancy. Treat at about 3 cm or more, and at smaller sizes in women who are or may become pregnant. Visceral pseudoaneurysms (after pancreatitis or trauma) are treated at any size, usually by embolization.
Renal artery stenosis two populations
Atherosclerotic (older patients, ostial lesions) and fibromuscular dysplasia (younger women, a "string of beads" in the mid vessel). Can cause renovascular hypertension and flash pulmonary edema. CORAL found no routine benefit from stenting atherosclerotic disease, so intervention is selective; FMD is treated with balloon angioplasty without a stent.
Fibromuscular dysplasia
A non-atherosclerotic arteriopathy of medium arteries, mainly renal and carotid or vertebral. It can cause dissections and aneurysms elsewhere, so patients are imaged from head to pelvis once.
Thoracic outlet syndrome three types
Neurogenic (most common, brachial plexus compression). Venous: Paget-Schroetter "effort thrombosis" of the subclavian vein, treated with thrombolysis then first rib resection. Arterial (rarest): subclavian aneurysm or stenosis from a cervical rib, with embolization to the hand. Decompression usually means first rib resection.
Subclavian steal the arm steals from the brain
A proximal subclavian stenosis causes reversed flow down the vertebral artery to feed the arm, giving posterior circulation symptoms with arm exercise and an arm-to-arm blood pressure difference. More common on the left.
Atheroembolism blue toe
Cholesterol crystals showered from an aortic or iliac plaque, often after a catheterization: painful blue toes with palpable pulses, livedo reticularis, sometimes kidney injury and eosinophilia. Find and treat the source, statin and antiplatelet; anticoagulation does not help.
Buerger disease young smoker, digits
Thromboangiitis obliterans: inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic occlusion of small and medium vessels in young smokers, causing digital ischemia and ulcers. The only treatment that works is complete abstinence from tobacco, including nicotine replacement in some patients.
Popliteal entrapment young athlete
An abnormal relationship between the popliteal artery and the gastrocnemius (or popliteus) compresses flow with exercise. Suspect it in a fit young person with calf claudication and normal pulses at rest that fade with plantar flexion.
Vascular trauma hard and soft signs
Hard signs (pulsatile bleeding, an expanding hematoma, a bruit or thrill, an absent pulse, distal ischemia) go to the OR. Soft signs (a diminished pulse, a stable hematoma, proximity to a vessel, a neurologic deficit) get CTA or an injured extremity index (ABI below 0.9 is abnormal).
Large-vessel vasculitis
Takayasu arteritis ("pulseless disease," younger women, aortic arch branches) and giant cell arteritis (over 50, temporal artery, jaw claudication, threat to vision, but also the subclavian and axillary arteries). Avoid operating during active inflammation.

The most common consults, with their first moves, are on Rounds and presenting.
