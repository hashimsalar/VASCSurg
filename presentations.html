Chapter 04 · The knowledge

Major presentations

The diseases that fill the consult list. For each one: what it is, how it shows up, how it is classified, and what is done about it. Read these in your first week, because they are most of what the service does and most of what you will be asked.

PAD & CLTI Acute limb ischemia Aneurysm Carotid Venous

Peripheral arterial disease & intermittent claudication

PAD is atherosclerotic narrowing of the limb arteries, sitting on a spectrum from asymptomatic, through intermittent claudication (reproducible exertional muscle pain relieved by rest), to chronic limb-threatening ischemia at the severe end. Claudication is a quality-of-life problem with a low limb-loss rate; the real danger to these patients is their coexisting coronary and cerebrovascular disease.

The two historical staging systems are worth knowing because attendings switch between them freely.

Management of claudication is medical first. Risk-factor therapy, a statin, an antiplatelet, and supervised exercise are the foundation. Revascularization is reserved for lifestyle-limiting symptoms that fail medical therapy, and even then the choice between endovascular and open repair depends on the lesion's location and length.

Chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI)

CLTI, the modern term for "critical limb ischemia," is the severe end of PAD, defined by ischemic rest pain lasting more than two weeks, non-healing ulceration, or gangrene, together with objectively reduced perfusion. Rest pain is classically in the forefoot and toes, worse lying flat at night, and relieved by hanging the foot off the bed, because gravity restores a trickle of perfusion.

Pearl · WIfI

The WIfI classification stages limb-threat risk along three axes: Wound, Ischemia (ABI or toe pressure), and foot Infection. It reframes the decision from "is there a pulse" to "what is this specific limb's risk of amputation and likely benefit from revascularization," which is how modern vascular surgeons actually think.

CLTI is limb-threatening and demands revascularization wherever feasible, by bypass or endovascular means, plus aggressive wound care and infection control. The landmark BEST-CLI trial showed that for patients with suitable great saphenous vein, open bypass gave better outcomes than an endovascular-first strategy, reviving the role of surgical bypass in the right patient.

Red flag

Untreated CLTI carries a high one-year risk of major amputation and death. Rest pain, a non-healing wound, or any gangrene is not something to observe; it is a referral for revascularization.

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) Emergency

ALI is the sudden loss of arterial perfusion threatening limb viability, classically within two weeks of onset. It is a true emergency, since muscle tolerates roughly 4 to 6 hours of ischemia before irreversible injury begins.

Mnemonic · the 6 Ps

Pain · Pallor · Pulselessness · Paresthesia · Paralysis · Poikilothermia (perishingly cold)

The order matters clinically, because paresthesia and paralysis are the ominous late signs. A limb with sensory or motor loss is on the clock; pain and pallor alone are earlier and more recoverable.

» Embolic vs thrombotic, the central distinction

Determining the mechanism changes the operation, so this comparison is asked relentlessly.

Question

Most common source of an arterial embolus, and most common site it lodges?

The heart is the usual source (most often atrial fibrillation). Emboli lodge at branch points where the vessel narrows; in the leg the common femoral bifurcation is the classic site.

» Management

  1. Anticoagulate immediately. Give a systemic heparin bolus and infusion the moment ALI is suspected, to prevent clot propagation, before any imaging.
  2. Assess viability (Rutherford class). The class decides the path: I and IIa allow time for imaging and possibly thrombolysis, IIb needs the operating room now, and III is non-salvageable.
  3. Revascularize. Options include catheter embolectomy with a Fogarty balloon (classic for embolic IIb), catheter-directed thrombolysis (often suited to thrombotic disease or category I to IIa), surgical bypass, or endovascular thrombectomy.
  4. Anticipate reperfusion. Restoring flow to ischemic muscle releases potassium, myoglobin, and acid. Watch for hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis, and compartment syndrome, and have a low threshold for fasciotomy.
Red flag · reperfusion injury

After revascularizing a limb ischemic for several hours, a tense, painful compartment with pain on passive stretch is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Pulses may still be present, so do not wait for them to disappear. The treatment is urgent four-compartment fasciotomy. Concurrently watch the potassium and the kidneys for rhabdomyolysis.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is a focal dilation greater than 1.5 times the normal vessel diameter, and for the infrarenal aorta that means a diameter at or above 3 cm. The overwhelming majority are infrarenal and degenerative. Risk factors are smoking (the strongest modifiable one), male sex, older age, family history, and other connective-tissue contributors.

Pearl · true vs false aneurysm

A true aneurysm involves all three layers of the wall (intima, media, adventitia). A pseudoaneurysm (false aneurysm) is a contained rupture, blood held in by only the outer layers or surrounding tissue, classically at an anastomosis or a femoral puncture site. The distinction is a frequent question.

» Screening & surveillance

The USPSTF recommends a one-time screening ultrasound for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked. Small aneurysms are followed by serial ultrasound at intervals that shorten as the aneurysm grows.

» When to repair (elective)

  • Diameter at or above 5.5 cm in men
  • Diameter at or above 5.0 cm in women
  • Rapid expansion beyond 0.5 cm in 6 months or 1 cm in a year
  • Symptomatic (tender aneurysm, distal embolization) at any size
Red flag · ruptured AAA

The classic triad is sudden abdominal or back and flank pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass. A hypotensive older patient with back pain is a ruptured AAA until proven otherwise. If unstable, the patient goes straight to repair, with no CT. Practice permissive (hypotensive) resuscitation, keeping the systolic pressure only high enough to perfuse the brain (around 70 to 90 mmHg while awake), because aggressive fluids and a higher pressure can blow out the contained rupture.

Question

At what size do you repair an asymptomatic infrarenal AAA in a man?

5.5 cm (5.0 cm in women), or sooner for rapid growth or symptoms. Below threshold, the rupture risk is lower than the operative risk, so you survey and optimize medical therapy.

Other variants to recognize are the inflammatory AAA (thickened, inflamed wall, often younger patients, elevated inflammatory markers, classic ureteral entrapment), and the mycotic (infected) aneurysm (saccular, rapidly expanding, febrile, often Salmonella or Staph).

Carotid disease & stroke prevention

Atherosclerotic plaque at the carotid bifurcation causes stroke by two mechanisms: embolization of plaque or thrombus to the brain (the more common mechanism) and, less often, flow limitation from a tight stenosis. The whole point of carotid intervention is stroke prevention, not symptom relief.

» Symptomatic vs asymptomatic

Symptomatic disease means an ipsilateral neurologic event in the prior 6 months: a TIA, a stroke, or amaurosis fugax (transient monocular blindness, "a curtain coming down," from a retinal-artery embolus). Asymptomatic disease is found on screening or a bruit.

Question

What is amaurosis fugax and what does it signify?

Transient, painless monocular vision loss from an embolus to the ophthalmic or retinal artery (a branch of the internal carotid). It is a form of TIA and marks the carotid as symptomatic, lowering the threshold to intervene.

» Indications, what the trials showed

Two landmark trial families set the thresholds. NASCET established the benefit of carotid endarterectomy in symptomatic patients, and ACAS and ACST did so for asymptomatic patients.

A near-total occlusion or a fully occluded (string-sign) carotid is generally not revascularized, because the risk outweighs the benefit.

» CEA vs CAS vs TCAR

Carotid endarterectomy remains the standard. Transfemoral carotid artery stenting is an option, particularly for high surgical risk or hostile necks, but the CREST trial showed stenting carried a higher periprocedural stroke risk while surgery carried a higher MI risk, with stroke risk rising in older patients undergoing stenting. Transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) uses direct carotid access with flow reversal for embolic protection and has become a popular middle path.

Red flag · timing after stroke

After a completed stroke, revascularization is generally done within about two weeks in stable patients (to prevent recurrence) but avoided in the hyperacute setting of a large infarct, where reperfusion can cause hemorrhagic transformation. Coordinate with neurology.

Mesenteric ischemia

Reduced blood flow to the gut, splitting into an acute, emergent form and a chronic, indolent one. The arteries at stake are the celiac trunk, the SMA, and the IMA.

» Acute mesenteric ischemia

The hallmark is pain out of proportion to the physical exam, a patient writhing in severe abdominal pain with a soft, relatively benign-feeling belly early on. There are four mechanisms.

Work-up shows leukocytosis, a rising lactate, and a metabolic acidosis, with CTA as the imaging test of choice. Management is to resuscitate, anticoagulate with heparin, give broad-spectrum antibiotics, perform urgent revascularization (embolectomy, bypass, or endovascular), and resect frankly necrotic bowel, with a planned second-look laparotomy at 24 to 48 hours to reassess marginal segments.

Question

Why does an SMA embolus spare the proximal jejunum?

The embolus typically lodges a few centimeters into the SMA, distal to the first jejunal branches and the middle colic origin, so the proximal jejunum (and sometimes the proximal colon) retains perfusion while the rest of the small bowel infarcts.

» Chronic mesenteric ischemia

This is "intestinal angina." The triad is postprandial abdominal pain, food fear (sitophobia), and weight loss, in an older arteriopath. Because of the rich collateral network, symptoms usually require significant disease in at least two of the three mesenteric vessels. Treatment is revascularization, increasingly endovascular (angioplasty or stent of the SMA).

Pearl · MALS

Median arcuate ligament syndrome is extrinsic compression of the celiac artery by the diaphragm's median arcuate ligament, classically in younger, thinner patients with postprandial pain and an epigastric bruit that changes with respiration. Treatment is surgical release of the ligament, not stenting.

Aortic dissection Emergency

A tear in the aortic intima lets blood track into the media, creating a false lumen that can propagate, occlude branch vessels (malperfusion), or rupture. The presentation is sudden, severe "tearing" chest or back pain, sometimes with a pulse or blood-pressure differential between limbs, or signs of organ malperfusion.

» Initial medical management (any type)

The goal is to reduce the shearing force on the aortic wall. Give an IV beta-blocker first (esmolol or labetalol) to bring the heart rate and contractility down, then add a vasodilator such as nitroprusside if the blood pressure is still high. Target a heart rate around 60 and a systolic around 100 to 120.

Red flag · sequence matters

Never give a vasodilator before beta-blockade. A vasodilator alone triggers reflex tachycardia, which raises the shearing force and can propagate the dissection. Beta-blocker first, always.

Type A dissections go to cardiac or vascular surgery emergently. Type B dissections are managed medically unless complicated by malperfusion, rupture, rapid expansion, or refractory pain or hypertension, in which case TEVAR is the usual intervention.

Deep vein thrombosis & VTE

Venous thromboembolism spans DVT and pulmonary embolism. The pathophysiology is captured by Virchow's triad.

Mnemonic · Virchow's triad

Stasis · Endothelial injury · Hypercoagulability

Every DVT risk factor (immobility, surgery, malignancy, oral contraceptives, inherited thrombophilia) maps onto one of these three.

Diagnosis combines pretest probability (the Wells score), D-dimer (useful to rule out when low-risk), and compression duplex ultrasound (the confirmatory test, showing a non-compressible vein). Treatment is anticoagulation; an IVC filter is reserved mainly for patients with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation or recurrent PE despite it.

Red flag · phlegmasia

Massive iliofemoral DVT can cause phlegmasia cerulea dolens, a swollen, blue, painful leg from near-total venous outflow obstruction, which can progress to venous gangrene and compartment syndrome. It is a limb-threatening emergency that may need thrombolysis or thrombectomy, not anticoagulation alone. Its milder, white-leg precursor is phlegmasia alba dolens.

Pearl · May-Thurner

May-Thurner syndrome is compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery against the spine, predisposing to left-leg DVT, classically in younger women. It is the reason an "unprovoked" left iliofemoral DVT deserves a look at the iliac vein, often treated with thrombolysis plus venous stenting.

Chronic venous insufficiency & varicose veins

When venous valves fail, blood refluxes and venous pressure stays high (venous hypertension), producing varicosities, leg heaviness, edema, skin changes, and ultimately ulceration. Severity is graded by the CEAP classification.

Mnemonic · CEAP

Clinical · Etiologic · Anatomic · Pathophysiologic

The clinical axis runs C0 (no signs), C1 (telangiectasia or reticular), C2 (varicose veins), C3 (edema), C4 (skin changes: pigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis), C5 (healed ulcer), and C6 (active venous ulcer).

Red flag

Never apply compression to a leg you have not confirmed has adequate arterial inflow. Compressing an ischemic (arterial) leg can precipitate tissue loss. Check the ABI before wrapping a "venous-looking" ulcer.

Management of chronic venous insufficiency starts with compression therapy and leg elevation. Definitive options for refluxing superficial veins include endovenous thermal ablation (radiofrequency or laser), sclerotherapy, and phlebectomy.

The diabetic foot

Diabetic foot disease is the convergence of three processes: peripheral neuropathy (loss of protective sensation leading to unnoticed trauma), ischemia (PAD, often tibial or below-knee with sparing of the foot vessels), and infection. Each worsens the others, and together they make the diabetic foot the leading cause of non-traumatic amputation.

  • Neuropathic ulcers classically sit under pressure points (metatarsal heads, heel) in a foot with intact but unfelt circulation.
  • Charcot foot is progressive neuropathic joint destruction producing the collapsed "rocker-bottom" deformity; in the acute phase it can mimic infection (red, hot, swollen) without a wound.
  • Severity is staged with systems such as Wagner and the University of Texas classifications.
Pearl

Management is multidisciplinary and rests on the same logic as the rest of the service: offload pressure, debride and control infection, and revascularize if there is ischemia. A "probe-to-bone" positive ulcer suggests osteomyelitis. Because diabetic disease favors the tibial vessels with patent pedal targets, distal bypass or pedal-loop angioplasty can salvage feet that look unsalvageable.

Other key presentations

Briefer, but each is a known consult and a known question.

Popliteal aneurysm
Clots, not ruptures

The most common peripheral aneurysm. The danger is thrombosis and distal embolization (limb loss), not rupture. Frequently bilateral and associated with AAA, so check the other leg and the aorta.

Thoracic outlet
Three flavors

Neurogenic (most common, brachial plexus). Venous, the Paget-Schroetter "effort thrombosis" of the subclavian vein. Arterial (rarest), from a cervical rib. Decompression often means first-rib resection.

Subclavian steal
Arm steals from brain

Proximal subclavian stenosis causes retrograde flow down the vertebral artery to feed the arm, producing posterior-circulation symptoms with arm exertion. More common on the left.

Renal artery stenosis
Two populations

Atherosclerotic (older, ostial) and fibromuscular dysplasia (younger women, "string of beads" mid-vessel). Causes renovascular hypertension and flash pulmonary edema.

Splenic artery aneurysm
Most common visceral

The commonest splanchnic artery aneurysm, with a notable risk of rupture in pregnancy and a female predominance. Treat at smaller sizes in women of childbearing age.

Buerger's disease
Young smoker, digits

Thromboangiitis obliterans, a non-atherosclerotic inflammatory occlusion of small and medium vessels in young male smokers, causing digital ischemia. The only real treatment is absolute smoking cessation.

Popliteal entrapment
Young athlete, no risk factors

An abnormal relationship of the popliteal artery to the gastrocnemius compresses flow with exertion. Suspect it in a fit young person with claudication and normal arteries at rest.

Vascular trauma
Hard vs soft signs

Hard signs (pulsatile bleeding, expanding hematoma, bruit or thrill, absent pulse, ischemia) go to the OR. Soft signs get imaging (CTA) and observation.

Large-vessel vasculitis
Two named patterns

Takayasu ("pulseless disease," young Asian women, aortic-arch branches). Giant cell arteritis (over 50, temporal artery, jaw claudication, vision-threatening).
