Perfusion and chronic staging

How to grade a leg with chronic arterial disease. Explained in Foundations and Presentations.

Ankle-brachial index
ABIInterpretation
> 1.40Non-compressible, calcified vessels (diabetes, kidney failure); use toe pressures
1.00–1.40Normal
0.91–0.99Borderline
0.41–0.90Mild to moderate PAD (the usual claudication range)
≤ 0.40Severe PAD; rest pain and tissue loss are common
TBI < 0.70Abnormal toe-brachial index; use it when the ABI is unreliable

A toe pressure (or TcPO2) under 30 mmHg predicts poor wound healing. When the resting ABI is normal but the story is claudication, an exercise ABI that falls by more than about 20 percent supports PAD.

Fontaine and Rutherford (chronic ischemia)
FontaineRutherfordClinical
I0Asymptomatic
IIa1Mild claudication (walks more than about 200 m)
IIb2–3Moderate to severe claudication (under about 200 m)
III4Ischemic rest pain
IV5–6Tissue loss: minor (5, ulcer or focal gangrene) or major (6, beyond the transmetatarsal level)

Rutherford 4 to 6 (Fontaine III and IV) is chronic limb-threatening ischemia, staged with WIfI below.

WIfI at a glance

The SVS threatened limb classification used by the Global Vascular Guidelines to stage CLTI. Grade wound, ischemia and foot infection 0 to 3 each; the combination maps to clinical stages 1 to 4, which estimate the one-year amputation risk and the likely benefit of revascularization. Use a WIfI chart or calculator for the stage. Explained in Presentations.

W: wound
GradeWound
0No ulcer and no gangrene (ischemic rest pain only)
1Small, shallow ulcer on the distal leg or foot; no exposed bone unless limited to the distal phalanx; no gangrene
2Deeper ulcer with exposed bone, joint or tendon, not involving the heel, or a shallow heel ulcer without calcaneal involvement; gangrene limited to the digits
3Extensive deep ulcer of the forefoot or midfoot, or a deep full-thickness heel ulcer with or without calcaneal involvement; extensive gangrene of the forefoot or midfoot, or full-thickness heel necrosis
I: ischemia
GradeABIAnkle pressureToe pressure or TcPO2
0≥ 0.80> 100 mmHg≥ 60 mmHg

20.40–0.5950–70 mmHg30–39 mmHg

When the vessels are calcified and the ABI or ankle pressure is unreliable, grade ischemia by toe pressure or TcPO2.

fI: foot infection
GradeFoot infection
0No signs of infection
1Local infection of skin and subcutaneous tissue only; erythema 0.5 to 2 cm around the wound
2Local infection with erythema over 2 cm, or involving deeper structures (abscess, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, fasciitis); no systemic signs
3Local infection with systemic inflammatory signs: two or more of temperature over 38 or under 36 °C, heart rate over 90, respiratory rate over 20 (or PaCO2 under 32 mmHg), white count over 12,000 or under 4,000 (or 10 percent bands)

Infection is present when there are at least two of: local swelling or induration, erythema, tenderness or pain, warmth, and purulent discharge. Arterial anatomy is staged separately with GLASS, which grades the femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal disease along the target path.

Mnemonic: the 6 Ps

Pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia. Paresthesia and paralysis are the late, ominous signs. Start heparin the moment acute limb ischemia is suspected, unless it is contraindicated.

Aorta and carotid

Repair thresholds, dissection and carotid revascularization. Explained in Presentations (aneurysm, dissection and carotid).

Aortic dissection
StanfordDeBakeyInvolvesDefault
AIAscending, arch and descendingEmergency surgery

Complicated type B means rupture or malperfusion; most definitions add refractory pain or hypertension and rapid expansion. Anti-impulse targets (2022 ACC/AHA) are a heart rate of 60 to 80 and a systolic pressure under 120 mmHg, beta-blocker first; older teaching used a heart rate under 60. The SVS/STS reporting standards time a dissection as hyperacute (under 24 hours), acute (1 to 14 days), subacute (15 to 90 days) or chronic, and describe its extent using the aortic zones below.

Carotid revascularization

Intervention assumes a perioperative stroke and death rate under about 6 percent for symptomatic and 3 percent for asymptomatic patients. Duplex grading is in Duplex criteria; CREST-2 is in Landmark trials.

Aortic zones, surveillance and endoleaks

The vocabulary of TEVAR planning, aneurysm follow-up and the post-EVAR scan. Explained in Endovascular and Open operations.

Arch landing zones (Ishimaru)
ZoneLocationLanding here means
0Ascending aorta, up to and including the innominate originAll arch branches covered; needs debranching or a branched or total arch repair
1Between the innominate and left common carotid, covering the left carotid originLeft carotid (and usually left subclavian) revascularization
2Between the left common carotid and left subclavian, covering the left subclavian originLeft subclavian covered; usually revascularized electively
3Proximal descending thoracic aorta just beyond the left subclavianNo arch branch covered
4Descending thoracic aorta beyond zone 3No arch branch covered

Left subclavian revascularization is essential when the left internal mammary feeds a coronary graft, the left vertebral is dominant, or there is a left arm dialysis access. The SVS/STS reporting standards continue the numbering down the aorta to the external iliac (zones 5 to 11).

Duplex criteria

Velocity thresholds for the two duplex reports you will read most. Criteria vary by lab and by machine, and accredited labs validate their own, so quote the category on the report and check your lab's criteria before quoting a number.

Internal carotid stenosis (SRU consensus, 2003)
StenosisICA peak systolic velocitySupporting findings
Normal< 125 cm/sNo visible plaque; ICA/CCA ratio < 2.0; EDV < 40 cm/s
< 50%< 125 cm/sVisible plaque; ratio < 2.0; EDV < 40 cm/s
50–69%125–230 cm/sRatio 2.0–4.0; EDV 40–100 cm/s
≥ 70%, short of near occlusion> 230 cm/sRatio > 4.0; EDV > 100 cm/s
Near occlusionHigh, low or undetectableMarkedly narrowed lumen on color Doppler; ratio and EDV unreliable
OcclusionUndetectableNo patent lumen

Peak systolic velocity and visible plaque are the primary criteria; the ratio and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) support them. A contralateral occlusion raises velocities on the open side, and stented arteries need their own, higher thresholds.

Infrainguinal vein graft surveillance
CategoryDuplex findingsAction
NormalPSV < 180 cm/s, velocity ratio < 2.0, mid-graft velocity > 45 cm/sRoutine surveillance
Moderate stenosisPSV 180–300 cm/s, ratio > 2.0Repeat duplex in weeks; intervene if progressing
High riskPSV > 300 cm/s, ratio > 3.5, mid-graft velocity < 45 cm/s, or ABI drop > 0.15Angiography and repair before the graft occludes

The velocity ratio compares the peak systolic velocity at the lesion with the segment just upstream. Vein graft surveillance commonly runs at a baseline study soon after surgery, then about 3, 6 and 12 months, then yearly; a failing graft is far easier to save than a failed one. In a native artery, a ratio of 2.0 or more generally means a stenosis of 50 percent or more.

Red flag

Confirm arterial inflow with an ABI before compressing any leg, because compressing an ischemic limb can cause tissue loss. Full compression is generally used when the ABI is above about 0.8; reduced compression may be used down to about 0.5 under specialist supervision, and compression is avoided below that. Thresholds vary between guidelines.

Scores

Three scores you will be asked to calculate on the spot: DVT probability, HIT probability and cardiac risk before surgery.

Wells score for DVT
CriterionPoints

A score of 2 or more means DVT is likely: get a duplex. Under 2 means unlikely: a negative high-sensitivity D-dimer rules it out without imaging.

4T score for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia
T2 points1 point
ThrombocytopeniaFall over 50% and nadir 20 or more (×10⁹/L)Fall of 30–50%, or nadir 10–19
TimingClear onset on days 5–10, or within 1 day if heparin was given in the past 30 daysProbably days 5–10 but unclear, onset after day 10, or within 1 day with heparin 30–100 days ago
ThrombosisNew proven thrombosis, skin necrosis at injection sites, or a systemic reaction to an IV heparin bolusProgressive or recurrent thrombosis, red skin lesions, or suspected but unproven thrombosis
Other causeNone apparentPossible

Score 0 for a T when neither column fits (for example a fall under 30%, a fall before day 4 with no recent heparin, or a definite other cause). Total 0 to 3 is low probability, 4 to 5 intermediate and 6 to 8 high. At intermediate or high probability, stop all heparin, start a non-heparin anticoagulant and send the PF4 immunoassay.

Two or more points means elevated risk, which prompts an assessment of functional capacity, with further testing only if it would change management. The RCRI tends to underestimate risk in vascular surgery patients, and vascular-specific tools exist.

Anticoagulant reversal at a glance

What reverses what, for bleeding or an urgent operation. Doses are the commonly taught ranges; the hospital pharmacy protocol governs. Explained in Foundations.

Reversal by agent
AgentReversalPractical points
Unfractionated heparinProtamine about 1 mg per 100 units given in the last 2 to 3 hours; maximum about 50 mg per doseGive slowly: hypotension, bradycardia and anaphylaxis, more likely with prior protamine, NPH insulin or fish allergy. Recheck ACT or aPTT.
EnoxaparinProtamine 1 mg per 1 mg enoxaparin within 8 hours of the dose; 0.5 mg per 1 mg at 8 to 12 hoursOnly partial reversal of anti-Xa activity
WarfarinVitamin K 5 to 10 mg IV plus 4F-PCC (dosed by INR and weight, or a fixed dose, per protocol)PCC works in minutes, vitamin K over hours; plasma only if PCC is unavailable
Apixaban, rivaroxabanAndexanet alfa or 4F-PCC (often about 50 units/kg), per local protocolChoice and availability vary. Time of last dose and renal function decide whether reversal is needed.
DabigatranIdarucizumab 5 g IVAlso removed by hemodialysis
Argatroban, bivalirudinNone; stop the infusionShort half-lives: argatroban about 45 minutes (liver), bivalirudin about 25 minutes (kidney)
Alteplase (catheter-directed lysis)Stop the infusion; cryoprecipitate and an antifibrinolytic per protocolCheck fibrinogen; stop any heparin running with it
Aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitorsNo specific reversalPlatelet transfusion only for life-threatening bleeding or urgent surgery, per protocol; it works poorly against ticagrelor

4F-PCC is four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate.

Confirm locally

Which reversal agents are stocked, how PCC is dosed, and who must approve andexanet or idarucizumab vary by hospital. Check the pharmacy protocol in PowerChart before you need it at 2 a.m.

Resident tip

Before you call about reversal, know five things: the drug, the dose, the time of the last dose, the creatinine clearance and where the bleeding is. Those decide whether reversal is needed at all, and they are the first questions the senior and the pharmacist will ask.

Conduits and access

The defaults worth stating without hesitation. Explained in Open operations (bypass and dialysis access).

Bypass conduit, in order of preference
ConduitWhen and why
Ipsilateral great saphenous veinFirst choice for infrainguinal bypass, especially below the knee; single segment, generally at least about 3 mm; reversed, non-reversed or in situ
Contralateral great saphenous veinNext, unless the other leg is likely to need its own bypass
Arm vein, small saphenous or spliced veinWhen the saphenous vein is absent or inadequate; spliced vein still beats prosthetic below the knee
Prosthetic (PTFE, Dacron)Acceptable above the knee and for aortic and iliac reconstruction; poor patency to below-knee and tibial targets
Dialysis access, in order of preference
AccessNotes
Radiocephalic fistula (Brescia-Cimino)Wrist; non-dominant arm and most distal site first
Brachiocephalic fistulaElbow; when the forearm vessels are unsuitable
Brachiobasilic fistula with transpositionThe deep basilic vein is brought to the surface, in one or two stages
Arteriovenous graftWhen no vein is suitable; usable sooner (about 2 to 3 weeks, or within days for early-cannulation grafts) but lower long-term patency and more infection
Tunneled catheterLast resort or bridge; highest infection and central venous stenosis risk

Fistula first, with a nuance: an autogenous fistula has the best long-term patency and lowest infection rate, but the 2019 KDOQI guideline frames access as an individualized life plan (the right access for the right patient at the right time), so a graft can be the better choice for some. Typical mapping minimums are a vein of about 2.5 mm and an artery of about 2 mm, though thresholds vary. Save the veins: no IVs, blood draws or PICCs in the non-dominant arm of a patient with advanced kidney disease.

Mnemonic: the Rule of 6s

A fistula is ready by about 6 weeks: flow over 600 mL/min, diameter over 6 mm, depth under 6 mm, and about 6 cm of straight vein to cannulate.

Landmark trials in one line each

The headline result of each trial you will hear named on rounds. These are summaries for orientation; the details, subgroups and limitations matter when you apply them.

Carotid
TrialHeadline
NASCET (1991, 1998)Symptomatic stenosis: endarterectomy sharply reduced ipsilateral stroke at 70–99% (about 26% to 9% at 2 years), gave a modest benefit at 50–69%, and none below 50%.
ACAS (1995)Asymptomatic stenosis of 60% or more: endarterectomy roughly halved the projected 5-year ipsilateral stroke risk (about 11% to 5%), with very low perioperative risk.
ACST-1 (2004, 2010)Asymptomatic severe stenosis: immediate endarterectomy roughly halved 5-year stroke risk compared with deferral, and the benefit lasted to 10 years in patients under 75.
CREST (2010)Stenting versus endarterectomy: similar overall composite outcome; more periprocedural strokes with stenting, more MIs with endarterectomy, and older patients did better with endarterectomy.
CREST-2 (2025)Asymptomatic stenosis of 70% or more, against intensive medical therapy alone: adding stenting lowered the rate of stroke or death; adding endarterectomy did not show a statistically significant benefit. Event rates were low in every arm.
Aneurysm
TrialHeadline
EVAR-1 (2004 to 2016)AAA of 5.5 cm or more, fit for open repair: EVAR had lower 30-day mortality, but the survival advantage was lost within a few years, with more reinterventions and higher late aneurysm-related mortality.
DREAM (2004, 2010)AAA of 5 cm or more: EVAR had lower early mortality and complications, with similar long-term survival and more reinterventions.
OVER (2009 to 2019)US veterans: EVAR had lower perioperative mortality, and long-term survival was similar to open repair.
Peripheral arterial disease and antithrombotics
TrialHeadline
BASIL (2005)Severe limb ischemia: bypass-first and angioplasty-first had similar amputation-free survival; patients who lived beyond 2 years appeared to do better with bypass first.
BEST-CLI (2022)CLTI suitable for either approach: with an adequate single-segment great saphenous vein, bypass reduced major adverse limb events or death compared with endovascular therapy; without it, outcomes were similar.
BASIL-2 (2023)CLTI needing infrapopliteal revascularization: an endovascular-first strategy had better amputation-free survival than vein bypass first, driven mainly by fewer deaths.
COMPASS (2017)Stable coronary or peripheral artery disease: low-dose rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily) plus aspirin reduced cardiovascular death, stroke and MI, and major limb events in PAD, with more major bleeding.
VOYAGER PAD (2020)After lower-extremity revascularization: rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily plus aspirin reduced acute limb ischemia, major amputation, MI, ischemic stroke and cardiovascular death combined, with more bleeding.
Venous
TrialHeadline
EVRA (2018)Venous leg ulcers with superficial reflux: early endovenous ablation plus compression healed ulcers faster than compression with deferred ablation.
ATTRACT (2017)Acute proximal DVT: adding pharmacomechanical catheter-directed thrombolysis to anticoagulation did not prevent post-thrombotic syndrome and increased major bleeding, though it reduced its severity.

Numbers worth memorizing

The figures that come up fast and are expected to be reflexive. Thresholds that already live in a table above (ABI, aneurysm repair, carotid, the Rule of 6s, protamine) are not repeated here.

  • 65 to 75AAA screening age: one-time ultrasound in men who have ever smoked (the SVS adds women who have smoked)
  • 50% / 75%Critical stenosis: about 50 percent diameter equals about 75 percent cross-sectional area
  • Flow ∝ r⁴Poiseuille: halve the radius and flow falls about sixteenfold
  • T ∝ P × rLaplace: wall tension climbs as an aneurysm grows
  • 80–100 U/kgIntraoperative heparin before clamping, then an ACT above roughly 250 seconds
  • 80 U/kg, 18 U/kg/hCommon weight-based heparin bolus and infusion for VTE, per local nomogram
  • 4–6 hoursWarm ischemia skeletal muscle tolerates before irreversible injury
  • < 30 mmHgCompartment perfusion gradient (diastolic minus compartment pressure) that supports fasciotomy
  • > 20 mmHgSustained bladder pressure that, with new organ dysfunction, defines abdominal compartment syndrome
  • Day 5–10Typical onset of HIT after heparin starts; sooner with recent exposure
  • < 90 minutesSVS door-to-intervention target for a ruptured AAA
  • T12 to L4Celiac T12, SMA L1, renals L1 to L2, IMA L3, bifurcation about L4
  • 2 of 3Chronic mesenteric ischemia usually needs significant disease in at least two of the celiac, SMA and IMA
  • 1 Fr = 0.33 mmSo 3 French is about 1 mm. Catheters are sized by outer diameter, sheaths by inner
  • 0.035 / 0.018 / 0.014Wire platforms in inches: workhorse, small vessel, tibial
