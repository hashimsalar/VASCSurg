Wires, catheters and devices

The hand tools of endovascular surgery, by name, so that you can follow what is being asked for in the angio suite or the hybrid OR and hand over the right thing. The concepts behind them are in the endovascular playbook; this page is the catalogue.

Confirm locally

Every hospital's shelf is different, and brand names below are examples of each category rather than a complete list or a recommendation. Spend ten minutes with the angio suite or hybrid OR tech in your first week learning which wires, catheters, sheaths and closure devices are stocked and where they live.

Sizes and fit

Three measuring systems are used at once, and almost every "will it fit?" question comes down to reading them correctly.

French (Fr)
Diameter, where 1 Fr is about 0.33 mm, so 3 Fr is about 1 mm and 6 Fr about 2 mm. A catheter's French is its outer diameter. A sheath's French is its inner diameter, the largest device it accepts, so its outer diameter (and the hole in the artery) is about 1.5 to 2 Fr bigger. A guide catheter is sized by its outer diameter, so a 6 Fr guide passes roughly what a 5 Fr sheath does.
Inches
Wires and the lumens they pass through. The workhorse platform is 0.035 inch; 0.018 and 0.014 inch platforms are used below the knee, in the renal and visceral branches, and with low-profile devices. A device labelled "0.035" accepts a wire up to that size.
Gauge (G)
Needles, where a higher number is smaller. An 18 G needle takes a 0.035 or 0.038 wire; a 21 G micropuncture needle takes a 0.018 wire.
Length (cm)
Working length of wires, catheters, sheaths and devices. To exchange one catheter for another over a wire without losing position, the wire must be roughly twice as long as the catheter (a 260 or 300 cm "exchange-length" wire). Devices for the contralateral tibials need long shafts (often 150 cm or more).

The compatibility chain

The needle must accept the wire; the wire must pass through the catheter or device; the catheter or device must pass through the sheath's inner diameter; and the sheath's outer diameter determines the hole in the artery and whether the access vessel is big enough. Read every package label in that order.

Over the wire or rapid exchange

Over-the-wire devices carry the wire through their whole length: better pushability and the ability to swap wires, but they need a long wire. Rapid-exchange (monorail) devices let the wire exit the shaft a short distance from the tip: a short wire is enough and exchanges are fast, but pushability is less. A balloon label such as "6 x 100 mm, 0.035, 135 cm, 6 Fr" means a 6 mm diameter by 100 mm length, a 0.035 wire, a 135 cm shaft, and a 6 Fr sheath.

Resident tip

When the attending asks for something, say it back with the size before you open it: "a 6 by 100 Mustang, 135 length?" Opened devices cannot be returned, and the read-back catches most mistakes. Write down every implant (lot sticker) for the brief op note.

Needles and micropuncture

Access is the first step and the commonest source of complications, which is why most operators now start small and use ultrasound.

Micropuncture kit

For example Cook Micropuncture, Merit MAK

  • 21 G needle
  • 0.018 wire
  • 4 to 5 Fr dilator

A small needle and wire, then a coaxial transitional dilator whose inner cannula is removed to accept a 0.035 wire. A misplaced stick leaves a small hole that is easy to hold.

When you'll hear it: "Micropuncture and the ultrasound, please."

18 G access needle

Generic (front-wall or Seldinger needle)

  • 18 G
  • 0.035 or 0.038 wire

The traditional single-step needle that takes a standard wire directly. Larger hole if the stick is wrong, so less used where micropuncture is available.

When you'll hear it: in emergencies and for large, easy targets.

Ultrasound guidance for the femoral, radial, brachial and pedal arteries reduces high and low sticks, back-wall punctures and accidental venous entry. Confirm the needle enters the common femoral artery over the femoral head, then check the position on fluoroscopy before upsizing. The access anatomy is covered in Endovascular.

Sheaths

A sheath holds the puncture open, has a one-way hemostatic valve and a side port for flushing or pressure, and lets you exchange catheters and devices without losing access or bleeding.

Short introducer sheath

For example Terumo Pinnacle, Cordis Avanti+

  • 4 to 8 Fr
  • about 10 to 11 cm

The standard first sheath for diagnostic work and ipsilateral iliac or femoral intervention.

When you'll hear it: "Put in a 5 French short sheath."

Long crossover sheath

For example Terumo Destination, Cook Flexor Ansel

  • 5 to 8 Fr
  • 45 to 90 cm
  • curved tip

Goes up and over the aortic bifurcation to sit in the opposite iliac or femoral artery, giving support and a short path for contralateral work. Placed over a stiff wire.

When you'll hear it: "Let's get a 6 French Destination up and over."

Straight long sheath

For example Cook Flexor Raabe

  • 5 to 8 Fr
  • straight

A long, straight sheath for antegrade access, renal or visceral work, or when extra support is needed without a crossover curve.

When you'll hear it: during antegrade femoral or visceral interventions.

Radial sheath

For example Terumo Glidesheath Slender

  • thin wall
  • hydrophilic

A thin-walled sheath that gives a larger inner diameter for a smaller outer diameter, reducing radial spasm and occlusion. Usually paired with an antispasmodic and heparin.

When you'll hear it: radial access for visceral or some peripheral cases.

Large-bore sheath

For example Gore DrySeal Flex, Cook Check-Flo Performer

  • 12 to 26 Fr
  • valved

Delivers EVAR and TEVAR components and other big devices. The access vessels must be large enough, and the arteriotomy is closed with preclose sutures or a surgical repair.

When you'll hear it: on every aortic stent graft case.

Hemostatic valve adapter

Generic (Tuohy-Borst, Y-adapter)

  • on guides and catheters

A screw-down valve with a side arm that lets a wire or device pass through a guide catheter while you flush or inject.

When you'll hear it: "Tighten the Tuohy," when contrast or blood is leaking back.

Guidewires

The wire is the rail everything else rides on. Wires differ in diameter, length, stiffness, tip shape and coating, and each case usually moves from a soft crossing wire to a stiffer delivery wire.

0.035 inch wires

J-tip starter wire

Generic, comes in most sheath kits

  • 0.035
  • about 150 cm
  • 3 mm J

The curved tip deflects off plaque and branch origins rather than digging in, so it is a safe first wire into the iliacs.

When you'll hear it: "Run the J up into the aorta."

Bentson

For example Cook

  • 0.035
  • 145 to 180 cm
  • long floppy tip

A straight wire with a long, very soft distal segment that buckles rather than perforates. Used to go up through diseased iliacs and through existing stents.

When you'll hear it: "Give me a Bentson."

Glidewire

Terumo

  • 0.035 (also 0.018, 0.038)
  • 150 to 260 cm
  • angled or straight
  • hydrophilic

Slippery when wet, steerable with a torque device, and the go-to for crossing tight or tortuous lesions and selecting branches. Stiff versions exist. Too slippery to hold position for bulky devices, so it is usually exchanged.

When you'll hear it: "Angled Glide and a torque device." Keep it wet.

Glidewire Advantage

Terumo

  • 0.035 and 0.018
  • hydrophilic tip
  • supportive body

A hydrophilic distal segment on a stiffer, coated body, so it crosses like a Glidewire but gives more support for delivering balloons and stents.

When you'll hear it: "Let's cross with an Advantage."

Rosen

For example Cook

  • 0.035
  • 145 to 260 cm
  • tight 1.5 mm J
  • moderate stiffness

A workhorse support wire with a small J tip that sits safely in branch vessels. Common for delivering iliac and renal stents and crossover sheaths.

When you'll hear it: "Exchange for a Rosen."

Amplatz

For example Boston Scientific Amplatz Super Stiff; Cook Amplatz Extra-Stiff

  • 0.035
  • stiff body
  • short soft tip

Stiff enough to straighten tortuous iliacs and track large sheaths and devices. The short floppy tip means the tip position must be watched.

When you'll hear it: "Get me an Amplatz for the sheath."

Lunderquist

Cook

  • 0.035
  • extra-stiff
  • curved soft tip
  • 260 cm and longer

The very stiff rail for EVAR and TEVAR delivery. It is placed through a catheter, and its tip is kept in view, because it can injure the arch, the aortic valve or the ventricle if pushed.

When you'll hear it: "Lunderquist up to the arch."

Supra Core

Abbott

  • 0.035
  • supportive
  • flexible tip

A peripheral support wire, stiffer than a Rosen and softer than an Amplatz, used for delivering devices across tortuous segments.

When you'll hear it: during iliac and femoral interventions needing support.

Steerable 0.035 wires

For example Medtronic Wholey, Boston Scientific Magic Torque

  • 0.035
  • shapeable tip
  • torque response

Non-hydrophilic wires with good torque control, for steering into a branch while giving moderate support.

When you'll hear it: "Try a Wholey."

0.018 and 0.014 inch wires

Used for tibial and pedal vessels, renal and visceral branches, and low-profile devices. They come in families graded by tip load (grams of force to buckle the tip): low tip loads for tracking, high tip loads for penetrating chronic total occlusions.

0.018 workhorse

For example Boston Scientific V-18 ControlWire, Abbott Command 18

  • 0.018
  • shapeable tip
  • supportive body

Common for femoropopliteal and tibial work with 0.018 balloons and stents.

When you'll hear it: "Go down with a V-18."

0.014 workhorse

For example Abbott Command 14

  • 0.014
  • shapeable tip

For tibial and pedal vessels and small-vessel devices. Often used through a support catheter.

When you'll hear it: below the knee and in pedal loops.

CTO wires

For example Cook Approach CTO; Asahi peripheral wires such as Astato

  • 0.014 and 0.018
  • graded tip loads

Stiffer-tipped wires for penetrating the cap of a chronic total occlusion, escalated stepwise from lower to higher tip loads.

When you'll hear it: "Escalate to the 12 gram."

Red flag: wire etiquette

Never let go of the wire, and always know where its tip is. Wipe wires with wet gauze and keep hydrophilic wires wet. Never pull a hydrophilic wire back through a metal needle, because the needle can shear off the coating or the tip. When a wire is loose on the table, coil it and keep it in the bowl so it does not slide off the field.

Catheters

Catheters either deliver a large bolus of contrast (flush catheters) or hook into a particular branch (selective catheters). Each shape exists for a geometry.

Flush catheters

Pigtail

Generic; similar designs include Cook Universal Flush

  • 4 to 5 Fr
  • coiled tip
  • side holes

Sits in a large vessel and delivers contrast through many side holes without whipping or injuring the wall. Used for aortograms on a power injector.

When you'll hear it: "Pigtail at L1 for the aortogram."

Omni Flush

AngioDynamics

  • 4 to 5 Fr
  • reverse curve
  • side holes

A flush catheter whose curved tip also hooks the opposite common iliac, so the same catheter does the aortogram and then helps walk a wire up and over the bifurcation.

When you'll hear it: "Go up and over with the Omni."

Support and microcatheters

Support (crossing) catheters

For example Cook CXI, Philips Quick-Cross, Terumo NaviCross, Medtronic TrailBlazer, Boston Scientific Rubicon

  • 0.014 to 0.035
  • tapered tip
  • braided shaft

Low-profile catheters that run right up to the wire tip to give it backup for pushing through an occlusion, and that allow a wire exchange beyond a lesion without losing position.

When you'll hear it: "Bring the CXI up behind the wire."

Microcatheters

For example Terumo Progreat, Boston Scientific Renegade

  • about 2.4 to 2.8 Fr
  • through a 4 to 5 Fr catheter

Run coaxially through a selective catheter into small branches, mainly to deliver coils, plugs or particles for embolization.

When you'll hear it: during type II endoleak and bleeding embolizations.

You are in the right common femoral artery and need to treat the left SFA. What do you need to get there?

A flush catheter with a curve (such as an Omni Flush) or a reverse-curve catheter (SOS Omni or Simmons) to hook the left common iliac at the bifurcation, a hydrophilic wire to go up and over, then an exchange for a stiffer wire to carry a long crossover sheath into the left iliac or femoral artery.

Balloons

Angioplasty balloons are tracked over the wire, positioned across the lesion and inflated with an inflation device (an indeflator) that reads pressure in atmospheres.

Compliance
Non-compliant balloons hold their size at high pressure (for hard, calcified lesions). Semi-compliant balloons grow a little with pressure (the usual angioplasty balloon). Compliant balloons stretch like latex to fill whatever they are in, and are used to occlude the aorta or mold a stent graft, never for angioplasty.
Nominal pressure
The pressure at which the balloon reaches its labelled diameter.
Rated burst pressure
The highest pressure the manufacturer supports. Going past it risks rupture of the balloon and the vessel.
Sizing
Usually about 1:1 with the normal reference vessel diameter, over a length that covers the lesion.

Plain balloons (POBA)

For example Boston Scientific Mustang (0.035) and Sterling (0.018); Abbott Armada 35 and 18; BD Ultraverse

  • 0.035 or 0.018
  • 3 to 12 mm
  • 20 to 200+ mm

Plain old balloon angioplasty, for iliac, femoral and popliteal disease and for vessel preparation before stents or drug-coated balloons.

When you'll hear it: "Open a 5 by 100 Sterling."

Small-vessel balloons

For example Boston Scientific Coyote

  • 0.014
  • 1.5 to 4 mm
  • long lengths

Low-profile, long balloons for tibial and pedal arteries, often on 150 cm shafts.

When you'll hear it: below the knee in CLTI.

Drug-coated balloons

For example Medtronic IN.PACT Admiral, BD Lutonix, Boston Scientific Ranger

  • paclitaxel
  • femoropopliteal

Transfer an antiproliferative drug to the vessel wall to reduce restenosis, mainly in the femoropopliteal segment, after the lesion has been prepared with a plain balloon. A 2018 meta-analysis raised a late mortality signal for paclitaxel devices; after further data, the FDA concluded in 2023 that the evidence does not support excess mortality.

When you'll hear it: "Prep with a plain balloon, then the DCB."

Cutting and scoring balloons

For example Boston Scientific cutting balloon; Philips AngioSculpt

  • blades or nitinol scoring elements

Score the plaque in a controlled way so it dilates at lower pressure with less dissection: resistant, fibrotic or recurrent lesions, including vein graft and dialysis access stenoses.

When you'll hear it: on a stubborn waist that will not open.

Intravascular lithotripsy

Shockwave (for example M5+ and S4)

  • sonic pressure waves
  • low-pressure balloon

Emitters inside a balloon deliver pulses that crack deep and superficial calcium, so a heavily calcified vessel can then be dilated. Also used to prepare calcified iliacs for large-bore sheaths.

When you'll hear it: "This is too calcified; get the Shockwave."

Compliant aortic balloons

For example Cook Coda, Medtronic Reliant; ER-REBOA (Prytime Medical)

  • compliant
  • large diameter

Mold stent graft seal zones and junctions after deployment, or occlude the aorta for control of a rupture (REBOA or an occlusion balloon through a sheath).

When you'll hear it: "Molding balloon to the neck and the overlaps."

Stents

Self-expanding nitinol stents for segments that bend and get compressed; balloon-expandable stents for precise placement and radial strength; covered stents to seal. The comparison table is in Endovascular.

Self-expanding nitinol

For example Abbott Absolute Pro, BD LifeStent, Boston Scientific Innova

  • 0.035
  • 5 to 10 mm
  • long lengths

Spring open as the sheath is pulled back and recover their shape after being squashed, which suits the SFA and iliacs. Can foreshorten or jump on release, so they are deployed slowly.

When you'll hear it: "6 by 120 self-expander in the SFA."

Interwoven nitinol

Abbott Supera

  • braided wire
  • high crush resistance

A woven stent with high radial strength and flexibility, used in the distal SFA and popliteal artery. It needs good vessel preparation and careful deployment so it is neither stretched nor compressed.

When you'll hear it: across the knee joint or in heavy calcium.

Drug-eluting peripheral

For example Cook Zilver PTX, Boston Scientific Eluvia

  • paclitaxel
  • femoropopliteal

Self-expanding stents that release paclitaxel to reduce restenosis in the femoropopliteal segment.

When you'll hear it: for longer or recurrent SFA disease.

Balloon-expandable

For example Boston Scientific Express LD, Abbott Omnilink Elite

  • 0.035
  • precise
  • high radial force

Mounted on a balloon and expanded to size, landing exactly where placed. Used at ostia (common iliac origins, renal arteries) where precision and strength matter, not in segments that flex.

When you'll hear it: "Kissing balloon-expandable stents at the bifurcation."

Covered stents

Gore Viabahn (self-expanding); Gore Viabahn VBX (balloon-expandable); Getinge iCast (Advanta V12 outside the US); Bentley BeGraft

  • ePTFE or PTFE cover

Stents lined with graft material to exclude an aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm, seal a rupture or perforation, treat in-stent restenosis, and bridge to branch vessels in fenestrated and branched repairs.

When you'll hear it: "Get a covered stent ready" when a vessel ruptures during angioplasty.

Carotid stents and TCAR

TCAR: ENROUTE system (Silk Road Medical, now Boston Scientific). Transfemoral: for example Abbott Xact, Cordis Precise

  • self-expanding
  • embolic protection

TCAR uses direct carotid access with flow reversal through a femoral venous sheath for embolic protection. Transfemoral stenting uses a distal filter. Dual antiplatelet therapy is needed after any carotid stent.

When you'll hear it: "Flow reversal on," during TCAR.

Embolic protection filters

For example Medtronic SpiderFX, Abbott Emboshield NAV6, Boston Scientific FilterWire EZ

  • 0.014 or 0.018
  • filter basket

A basket on a wire placed beyond the lesion to catch debris during carotid stenting, atherectomy or some thrombectomies, then captured and removed.

When you'll hear it: before atherectomy in a single-vessel runoff.

Venous stents

For example BD Venovo, Boston Scientific Vici, Medtronic Abre, Cook Zilver Vena

  • large diameter
  • self-expanding

Dedicated stents for iliofemoral venous obstruction such as May-Thurner, sized with IVUS.

When you'll hear it: after thrombus removal for left iliac vein compression.

Aortic stent grafts

EVAR and TEVAR devices are modular: a main body, then limbs and extensions, assembled inside the patient through large-bore sheaths.

Main body
The proximal part that seals in the aortic neck below the renal arteries, with a short "contralateral gate" to be cannulated from the other groin.
Limbs and extensions
Iliac limbs complete the bifurcated graft; extensions lengthen a limb, and proximal cuffs extend the seal upward.
Fixation
Suprarenal fixation uses a bare stent with barbs above the renal arteries (for example Endurant and Zenith); infrarenal fixation anchors below them (for example Excluder).
Oversizing
Grafts are oversized relative to the neck, typically about 10 to 20 percent for aneurysms, so the fabric seals against the wall. Less oversizing is used in dissection.

EVAR grafts

Gore Excluder and Excluder Conformable; Medtronic Endurant II and IIs; Cook Zenith Flex; Endologix AFX2 and Alto; Terumo Aortic TREO

  • bifurcated
  • modular

The standard infrarenal devices. AFX2 is a unibody graft that sits on the native aortic bifurcation; Alto seals with a polymer-filled ring for short necks; Excluder Conformable can be angled to fit angulated necks.

When you'll hear it: named by brand at the planning conference, sized from the CTA.

Iliac branch device

For example Gore Excluder Iliac Branch Endoprosthesis

  • internal iliac preservation

Extends the repair into the common iliac while keeping the internal iliac perfused, avoiding buttock claudication and pelvic ischemia from coiling it.

When you'll hear it: with common iliac aneurysms.

Fenestrated and branched

For example Cook Zenith Fenestrated; Gore Excluder Thoracoabdominal Branch Endoprosthesis (FDA approved 2024)

  • custom or off-the-shelf
  • bridging stents

Holes (fenestrations) or side branches line up with the renal and mesenteric arteries, which are then connected with covered bridging stents. Physician-modified grafts are a further option at some centers.

When you'll hear it: for juxtarenal and thoracoabdominal aneurysms.

TEVAR grafts

Gore TAG and Conformable TAG; Medtronic Valiant Navion; Cook Zenith Alpha Thoracic; Terumo Aortic RelayPro. Branch: Gore TAG Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis

  • thoracic
  • tube grafts

Tube grafts for the descending thoracic aorta: aneurysms, complicated type B dissections, traumatic transections and penetrating ulcers. The thoracic branch device preserves the left subclavian when landing in zone 2.

When you'll hear it: with landing zone and left subclavian planning.

Pearl

Every aortic device has instructions for use with minimum neck length, diameter range, angulation limits and access vessel size. "Is this on-label?" is a fair question at the planning conference, and the answer comes from the CTA measurements, not the device brochure.

Thrombectomy and lysis

Tools for removing clot from arteries, grafts, veins and the pulmonary arteries, used alone or combined.

Fogarty embolectomy catheter

Edwards Lifesciences

  • 2 to 7 Fr
  • balloon tip
  • open or over-the-wire

Passed beyond the clot through an arteriotomy, inflated gently and withdrawn to drag clot out. Typical sizes are 2 to 3 Fr for tibial vessels, 3 to 4 Fr for the popliteal, 4 to 5 Fr for the femoral and SFA, and 5 to 6 Fr for the iliac; the full table is in Open operations. Inflate with saline by feel, not to a set pressure, to avoid injuring the intima.

When you'll hear it: "A 4 Fogarty down the popliteal."

Aspiration thrombectomy

For example Penumbra Indigo system with Lightning

  • continuous suction
  • several catheter sizes

Large-lumen catheters connected to continuous aspiration, increasingly used for acute limb ischemia and pulmonary embolism. Watch the blood loss in the canister.

When you'll hear it: "Let's aspirate first."

Rheolytic thrombectomy

Boston Scientific AngioJet

  • saline jets
  • can deliver lytic

High-speed saline jets fragment and aspirate clot, and can "power-pulse" a lytic into it. It causes hemolysis (red urine) and can cause bradycardia, so run times are limited and hydration matters.

When you'll hear it: with a dark red urine bag afterwards.

Mechanical venous and PE thrombectomy

Inari (now Stryker) ClotTriever and FlowTriever

  • large bore
  • no lytic needed

Coring and aspiration devices that remove large volumes of clot from iliofemoral veins (ClotTriever) and the pulmonary arteries (FlowTriever) without thrombolytics.

When you'll hear it: for extensive DVT or intermediate- to high-risk PE.

Lysis infusion catheters

For example Medtronic Cragg-McNamara, AngioDynamics Unifuse; ultrasound-assisted: Boston Scientific EKOS

  • multi-sidehole
  • infusion length options

Placed through the clot to infuse a low dose of thrombolytic over hours, usually in the ICU, with neuro checks, access-site checks and fibrinogen and hemoglobin monitoring per protocol.

When you'll hear it: "Leave a lysis catheter overnight and re-look in the morning."

Atherectomy

Devices that remove or modify plaque rather than just displacing it, used mainly in heavily calcified or bulky lesions and as vessel preparation.

Directional

Medtronic HawkOne

  • cuts and collects plaque

A rotating blade shaves plaque into a collection chamber in one direction at a time, steered toward the bulk of the disease.

Orbital

Diamondback 360 (Cardiovascular Systems, now Abbott)

  • eccentric diamond crown

An orbiting crown sands calcium, widening its orbit with speed. Used for calcified femoropopliteal and tibial disease.

Rotational with aspiration

Boston Scientific Jetstream

  • rotating tip
  • active aspiration

A front-cutting rotating tip with aspiration, which removes debris and some thrombus.

Laser

For example Philips Turbo-Elite, AngioDynamics Auryon

  • photoablation

Laser energy vaporizes plaque and thrombus, and is used for in-stent restenosis and some occlusions.

Image-guided directional

Avinger Pantheris

  • optical coherence tomography

A directional device with an imaging core that shows the vessel layers in real time while cutting.

Red flag: distal embolization

Atherectomy liberates debris. A foot that is worse after the case, or a lost runoff vessel on the completion angiogram, may be embolization; many operators use a filter in limited runoff. Compare the distal exam with the pre-procedure baseline.

Re-entry devices

When a wire crosses an occlusion in the subintimal plane and cannot get back into the true lumen beyond it, a re-entry device aims a needle or wire back in.

Fluoroscopically oriented

For example Cordis Outback Elite, Medtronic Enteer, Boston Scientific OffRoad

  • needle or balloon based

Oriented with markers on orthogonal fluoroscopic views, then a needle or wire is directed into the true lumen beyond the occlusion.

When you'll hear it: "We're subintimal and can't re-enter; open an Outback."

IVUS-guided

Philips Pioneer Plus

  • built-in IVUS

An intravascular ultrasound transducer shows the true lumen directly, so the needle is aimed under imaging rather than by fluoroscopic projection.

The alternative strategies, including retrograde pedal or tibial access, are described under planning in Endovascular.

Embolization tools and IVC filters

Sometimes the goal is to stop flow: a type II endoleak, a bleeding vessel, an internal iliac before an aortic repair, or a visceral aneurysm.

Coils

Pushable: for example Cook Nester and Tornado. Detachable: for example Boston Scientific Interlock, Medtronic Concerto, Penumbra Ruby

  • platinum or steel
  • with fibers

Pushable coils are cheaper and pushed out of the catheter; detachable coils can be positioned, withdrawn if wrong, and released precisely. Occlusion depends partly on the patient's ability to clot.

Vascular plugs

For example Abbott Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and IV; Medtronic MVP

  • nitinol mesh
  • single device

A self-expanding mesh plug that occludes a larger vessel with one device and precise placement, such as an internal iliac artery.

Liquid agents

n-butyl cyanoacrylate glue; ethylene vinyl alcohol (Medtronic Onyx)

  • through microcatheters

Fill a space or a network of vessels and do not depend on the patient's clotting, which helps in coagulopathic bleeding. Need experience to control.

Gelfoam and particles

Gelatin sponge; particles such as PVA and microspheres

  • temporary or distal

Gelfoam gives temporary occlusion (trauma bleeding); particles occlude small distal vessels (tumors, some bleeding).

IVC filters

Retrievable: for example BD Denali, Argon Option Elite, Cook Celect and Günther Tulip. Permanent: Greenfield, B. Braun VenaTech

  • infrarenal
  • jugular or femoral delivery

Catch emboli in a patient with VTE who cannot be anticoagulated. Retrievable filters should come out once anticoagulation is possible; long dwell times bring migration, perforation, fracture and caval thrombosis.

When you'll hear it: "Who owns the retrieval?" Put it in the discharge summary.

Closure devices

At the end of the case the arteriotomy is closed with a device or held with manual pressure. Device closure lets patients sit up and walk sooner but does not lower the overall complication rate in every setting.

Suture-mediated

Abbott Perclose ProGlide and ProStyle

  • suture closure
  • preclose for large bore

Deploys a pre-tied suture through the arterial wall around the arteriotomy. For large-bore sheaths, two devices are usually placed before the sheath goes in (the "preclose" technique) and tied at the end.

When you'll hear it: "Two ProGlides at ten and two."

Collagen anchor

Terumo Angio-Seal

  • intra-arterial anchor
  • collagen plug

A small absorbable anchor inside the artery and a collagen plug outside sandwich the hole. It absorbs over weeks to months, which limits early re-puncture at the same spot (see the instructions for use).

When you'll hear it: after diagnostic and small-sheath interventions.

Extravascular sealant

Cordis Mynx

  • polyethylene glycol sealant

A sealant deployed on the outside of the artery while a small balloon temporarily occludes the hole from inside, leaving nothing in the lumen.

Clip

Abbott StarClose SE

  • nitinol clip
  • extravascular

A small clip grips the outside of the arteriotomy and closes it.

Large-bore plug

Teleflex MANTA

  • large-bore arteriotomies

A collagen-based device designed for large-bore holes after aortic and structural heart procedures.

Radial compression band

Terumo TR Band

  • inflatable band

Compresses the radial puncture with just enough pressure for hemostasis while keeping the artery patent (patent hemostasis), deflated stepwise per protocol.

When you'll hear it: on the nursing handoff after radial cases.

Manual compression

Still the standard for many small sheaths. Pull the sheath when anticoagulation allows (an ACT threshold per protocol), then press firmly over the arteriotomy, which lies just above the skin puncture, for about 15 to 20 minutes, longer for bigger sheaths or anticoagulated patients. Check the distal pulses afterwards. Closure devices are generally avoided when the puncture is at or below the bifurcation, in a small or heavily calcified artery, or in an infected groin.

A patient has a hematoma and a falling hemoglobin four hours after a closure device was used in the groin. What do you check and who do you call?

Hold firm pressure over the arteriotomy, mark the hematoma edge, check the distal pulses and vitals, send a hemoglobin, and call the senior. Think about a retroperitoneal bleed if the puncture may have been high (hypotension and flank pain with a modest groin), a failed device, and a pseudoaneurysm. The playbook is on On call.

IVUS, CO2 and other adjuncts

Tools that help you see: inside the vessel, without iodine, or with a precisely timed contrast bolus.

IVUS catheters

For example Philips Visions PV; Boston Scientific OptiCross

  • 0.014 to 0.035 compatible

An ultrasound transducer on a catheter gives cross-sectional images from inside the vessel: true lumen versus false lumen, vessel diameter for sizing, and stent apposition. Indispensable in venous stenting and helpful in dissection and contrast-sparing work.

CO2 delivery system

Dedicated closed delivery systems

  • below the diaphragm only

A closed system delivers medical CO2 as a contrast agent without room-air contamination, for patients with renal impairment or contrast allergy. Not used above the diaphragm or in the arm arteries.

Power injector

Generic

  • rate (mL/s)
  • volume
  • pressure limit (psi)

Delivers a set volume at a set rate for aortograms and runoff studies. The operator sets the rate, total volume and pressure limit for the catheter in use; check the catheter's maximum pressure rating.

When you'll hear it: "15 for 30," meaning 15 mL per second for a total of 30 mL.

Resident tip

Keep a running total of the contrast volume and the fluoroscopy time and dose during the case, and put them in the brief op note. They matter for the creatinine check the next morning and for radiation follow-up.
