SECTION 00

Orientation & mindset

Before the facts, the frame. Vascular is a small, intense service with a logic of its own. Get the logic and the facts hang on it.

How to use this guide

Read sections 01 (Foundations) and 02 (Presentations) before your first day. They cover the language and the reasoning everyone will assume you have. Skim 03 (Operations) the night before you scrub a given case, then re-read the relevant entry after, while it is fresh. Section 04 (Getting Pimped) is for the ten minutes before rounds. Section 06 (Reference) is what you screenshot and keep on your phone.

The colored boxes recur throughout and each means something specific:

Pimp

A question you will actually be asked, with the answer an attending wants to hear. If you can answer the pimp boxes cold, you are ahead of most rotators.

Pearl

A clinical or operative point that separates someone who memorized a list from someone who understands the disease.

Red flag

A do-not-miss finding, an emergency, or a classic trap. These are the things that hurt patients and end rotations badly.

Mnemonic

A memory hook. Use the ones that stick and ignore the rest.

The vascular mindset

Three habits of thought will make you sound like you belong on the service.

First, everything reduces to inflow, outflow, and conduit. A leg, a graft, a bypass, a fistula, all of it. Is there enough blood arriving (inflow), a healthy bed for it to drain into (outflow, or runoff), and an intact channel between the two (conduit)? When a graft clots or a wound will not heal, the question is always which of those three failed.

Second, the vascular patient is a cardiac patient who happens to have a leg or carotid problem. Atherosclerosis is systemic. The person with claudication almost certainly has coronary disease too, which is why the leading cause of death after nearly every vascular operation is myocardial infarction rather than the operated territory itself. Optimize the heart, and treat the limb in that context.

Third, time is tissue. Acute limb ischemia, ruptured aneurysm, acute mesenteric ischemia, a cold mottled foot after a catheterization, these are clocks running against muscle, bowel, and brain. The reflex is to anticoagulate early, image fast, and call for help before the window closes.

Pearl

When you present a patient on this service, lead with the vascular bed, the symptom severity, the pulse exam, and the hemodynamics (ABI or duplex). An attending wants the limb characterized before the social history. Structure your thinking the way the service thinks and you will rarely be lost.

SECTION 01

Foundations & things to know

Anatomy, hemodynamics, the exam, and the pharmacology that underpin every case on the service.

Arterial anatomy, head to toe Memorize

You will be asked to name vessels in order, in the OR, while holding a retractor. Learn the path of blood from the aorta to the toes and from the arch to the fingertips.

» The aorta and its branches

The thoracic aorta gives the coronary arteries, then the three arch vessels, then descends. The classic arch order, right to left, is the brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk, the left common carotid, and the left subclavian. The brachiocephalic then splits into the right common carotid and right subclavian.

Below the diaphragm the abdominal aorta gives, in descending order, the celiac trunk (roughly T12), the superior mesenteric artery / SMA (L1), the paired renal arteries (L1–L2), the inferior mesenteric artery / IMA (L3), and finally bifurcates into the common iliac arteries at about L4.

Mnemonic

Arch order, R→L: "ABC's of the arch" — Arteria innominata (brachiocephalic), left common Carotid, left Subclavian. And the celiac trunk's three branches are the Left gastric, Splenic, Common hepatic.

» The lower extremity, in order

This is the single most pimped sequence in the field. Trace it until it is automatic:

  • Common iliac → splits into internal iliac (hypogastric), supplying the pelvis, and external iliac.
  • The external iliac passes under the inguinal ligament and becomes the common femoral artery (CFA).
  • The CFA bifurcates into the profunda femoris (deep femoral), the key collateral vessel of the thigh, and the superficial femoral artery (SFA).
  • The SFA travels down the thigh through the adductor (Hunter's) canal and, at the adductor hiatus, becomes the popliteal artery.
  • Below the knee the popliteal gives the anterior tibial artery (which becomes the dorsalis pedis on the foot) and then the tibioperoneal trunk, which splits into the posterior tibial (passes behind the medial malleolus) and the peroneal (fibular) artery.
Pimp

What are the three tibial ("trifurcation") vessels, and which two feed the pedal arch?

Anterior tibial, posterior tibial, and peroneal. The dorsalis pedis (continuation of the anterior tibial) and the posterior tibial form the pedal arch; the peroneal ends above the ankle and communicates with both but does not normally reach the foot directly.

Pimp

What runs with the artery in the adductor canal?

The femoral artery and vein, the saphenous nerve, and the nerve to vastus medialis. The saphenous nerve is the named structure they are fishing for.

» The upper extremity, in order

Subclavian → (under the clavicle, over the first rib) axillary → (past teres major) brachial → bifurcates at the elbow into the radial and ulnar arteries, which join in the hand as the superficial and deep palmar arches. This dual supply is what makes the Allen test meaningful and what lets a radial-cephalic fistula work.

Venous anatomy

Veins are divided into a deep system (paired with the named arteries, carrying ~85–90% of return), a superficial system, and the perforators that connect them. The two superficial veins you must know are the great saphenous vein (GSV), running up the medial leg and thigh to join the common femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction, and the small saphenous vein (SSV), running up the posterior calf to the popliteal vein.

Pearl

The GSV is the workhorse conduit of vascular and cardiac surgery: it is the preferred graft for infrainguinal bypass and for coronary bypass. Before you ablate or harvest it, someone always asks whether the patient might need it as a conduit later. A vein mapped and spared today can be a limb saved tomorrow.

Collateral pathways High yield

Collaterals explain why a patient can occlude a major vessel and still keep a viable limb or gut. Named collateral routes are favorite pimp material.

Circle of Willis
Anastomotic ring at the brain base linking the anterior (carotid) and posterior (vertebrobasilar) circulations. Why some patients tolerate carotid occlusion and others stroke.
Marginal artery of Drummond
Continuous arcade along the colon's mesenteric border connecting SMA and IMA territories.
Arc of Riolan
A shorter, more central SMA–IMA connection (the "meandering mesenteric artery"); enlarges dramatically when one mesenteric vessel occludes chronically.
Profunda femoris
The main collateral around an occluded SFA; geniculate branches reconstitute the popliteal. A good profunda can carry a limb despite a dead SFA.
Aortoiliac collaterals
In Leriche-type occlusion, flow reaches the legs via lumbar → iliolumbar and via IMA → superior rectal → middle/inferior rectal (internal iliac).

Hemodynamics & physiology

A handful of physical principles get asked again and again because they explain disease.

» Poiseuille's law

Flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius (Q ∝ r⁴) and inversely proportional to length and viscosity. The punchline: small changes in lumen diameter cause large changes in flow, which is why a focal stenosis can be so hemodynamically costly, and why a slightly larger conduit dramatically improves flow.

» Critical stenosis

A stenosis generally becomes hemodynamically significant once it narrows the diameter by about 50% (roughly 75% cross-sectional area), at which point a measurable pressure and flow gradient appears across it. This is the threshold duplex velocity criteria are designed to detect.

» Laplace's law and aneurysms

Wall tension rises with both pressure and radius (T ∝ P × r). As an aneurysm enlarges, wall tension climbs, which raises rupture risk and accelerates further dilation, a self-reinforcing loop that is the entire rationale for size-based repair thresholds.

Pimp

Why does aneurysm rupture risk rise so steeply with diameter?

Laplace's law: tension in the wall is proportional to pressure times radius, so a larger radius means higher wall stress at the same blood pressure, which both predisposes to rupture and drives continued expansion.

The vascular history & physical exam

The exam is where you earn credibility. Do it the same way every time.

» History: localizing claudication

The level of arterial disease predicts where the patient hurts when walking, because the pain occurs one level below the occlusion. Buttock and hip claudication points to aortoiliac disease, thigh claudication to the common femoral or iliac level, and the most common pattern, calf claudication, to the superficial femoral / popliteal segment.

Mnemonic — Leriche syndrome

Aortoiliac occlusive disease gives the triad of buttock/thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction, and absent femoral pulses. Picture the blockage sitting at the aortic bifurcation, starving everything downstream.

» The pulse exam

Palpate and grade every pulse, comparing sides. A common scale is 0 = absent, 1+ = diminished, 2+ = normal, 3+ = bounding (some services add a 4th grade). Document the sites: radial, brachial, carotid, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial. If you cannot feel a distal pulse, reach for the handheld Doppler and describe the signal as triphasic, biphasic, or monophasic.

Pearl

"Doppler signals" and "palpable pulses" are not the same thing, and saying so precisely matters. A monophasic Doppler signal in a foot that has no palpable pulse still tells you the foot is perfused, which can be the difference between watchful waiting and an emergent trip to the angio suite. Always state both what you felt and what you heard.

» Provocative and inspection findings

  • Buerger's test: elevation causes pallor in an ischemic limb; dependency causes a dusky dependent rubor. The angle at which pallor appears tracks severity.
  • Capillary refill > 2–3 seconds and loss of hair, shiny atrophic skin, and thickened nails suggest chronic ischemia.
  • Allen test before radial access or radial-artery harvest confirms the ulnar artery and palmar arch can supply the hand alone.
  • Auscultate for bruits over the carotids, abdomen, and femorals; a bruit signals turbulent flow across a stenosis.

Noninvasive testing & the ABI Core skill

The ankle-brachial index (ABI) is the bedside number that quantifies perfusion. Measure the higher of the two ankle systolic pressures (DP or PT) in each leg and divide by the higher of the two brachial pressures.

Red flag — the falsely reassuring ABI

A "normal" or high ABI in a diabetic or dialysis patient may be an artifact of medial calcinosis making the vessels incompressible. If the ABI is > 1.40 or does not match a clearly ischemic foot, get a toe-brachial index (TBI); the small digital arteries are usually spared from calcification. A TBI < 0.70 is abnormal, and toe pressure < 30 mmHg predicts poor wound healing.

» The rest of the noninvasive toolbox

  • Segmental limb pressures & pulse-volume recordings (PVR): cuffs at thigh, calf, and ankle localize the level of disease by where the pressure drops or the waveform flattens.
  • Exercise ABI: a normal resting ABI that falls after treadmill walking unmasks significant disease and confirms that leg symptoms are vascular.
  • Arterial duplex ultrasound: combines B-mode imaging with Doppler velocities; a rise in peak systolic velocity across a segment grades stenosis. The first-line tool for surveillance.

Imaging modalities

Duplex US
First-line, no contrast

Cheap, repeatable, operator-dependent. Workhorse for carotid, graft surveillance, AAA screening/follow-up, and vein mapping.

CTA
The anatomy roadmap

Fast and detailed; the go-to for aneurysm sizing, dissection, trauma, and operative planning. Costs are iodinated contrast and radiation.

MRA
Contrast-sparing option

Good soft-tissue detail without iodinated contrast or radiation. Slower; overestimates stenosis; gadolinium concerns in renal failure.

DSA (catheter angio)
The gold standard

Highest spatial resolution and the only one that's also therapeutic. Invasive, so reserved for when intervention is likely or other imaging is equivocal.

Pimp

What is the gold-standard imaging test for the arterial tree, and why isn't it first-line?

Catheter-based digital subtraction angiography (DSA). It is invasive and carries access-site, contrast, and radiation risk, so noninvasive duplex/CTA come first; angiography is chosen when you intend to treat in the same setting.

» Intraoperative heparin

Before clamping any artery, the surgeon gives systemic heparin, classically about 80–100 units/kg, then confirms an activated clotting time (ACT) above roughly 250 seconds before applying the clamp. At the end, heparin may be reversed with protamine.

Red flag — protamine reaction & HIT

Protamine, derived from fish sperm, can cause hypotension, bradycardia, and anaphylaxis, especially in patients with prior protamine exposure, fish allergy, or NPH-insulin use; give it slowly. And never forget heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT): an immune reaction (antibodies to the PF4–heparin complex) appearing ~5–10 days after exposure, causing a falling platelet count and paradoxical thrombosis. Stop all heparin, do not transfuse platelets, and anticoagulate with a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban, bivalirudin). Use the 4T score to gauge probability.

Pimp

A heparinized patient's platelets drop on day 7 and they clot a graft. Diagnosis and next step?

HIT. Stop every source of heparin (including flushes and coated catheters), start a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban, send HIT antibody / serotonin-release assay, and do not give platelet transfusions, which can worsen thrombosis.

Medical & risk-factor therapy

Revascularization treats the plumbing; medical therapy treats the disease. Every PAD patient should be on best medical therapy regardless of whether they get an operation.

  • Smoking cessation is the single highest-impact intervention, slowing progression and improving graft patency more than any drug.
  • High-intensity statin for every atherosclerotic patient, for plaque stabilization and cardiovascular risk reduction, not just lipids.
  • Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel); low-dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin is now used in selected PAD patients to reduce limb and cardiovascular events.
  • Blood pressure and diabetes control, and an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agent where indicated for cardiovascular benefit.
  • Supervised exercise therapy is first-line for claudication and rivals revascularization for walking distance.
  • Cilostazol (a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor) improves claudication distance but is contraindicated in heart failure.
Pimp

What is the most important intervention for a smoker with claudication?

Smoking cessation, paired with a statin, antiplatelet therapy, and a supervised exercise program. For pure claudication, medical therapy and exercise come before any consideration of intervention.

SECTION 02

Major presentations

The diseases that fill the consult list. For each: what it is, how it shows up, how it's classified, and what's done about it.

Peripheral arterial disease & intermittent claudication

PAD is atherosclerotic narrowing of the limb arteries, sitting on a spectrum from asymptomatic, through intermittent claudication (reproducible exertional muscle pain relieved by rest), to chronic limb-threatening ischemia at the severe end. Claudication is a quality-of-life problem with a low limb-loss rate; the real danger to these patients is their coexisting coronary and cerebrovascular disease.

The two historical staging systems are worth knowing because attendings switch between them freely.

Fontaine stages vs Rutherford categories (chronic ischemia)

Management of claudication is medical first. Risk-factor therapy, a statin, antiplatelet, and supervised exercise are the foundation. Revascularization is reserved for lifestyle-limiting symptoms that fail medical therapy, and even then the choice between endovascular and open repair depends on the lesion's location and length.

Chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI)

CLTI (the modern term for "critical limb ischemia") is the severe end of PAD, defined by ischemic rest pain lasting more than two weeks, non-healing ulceration, or gangrene, together with objectively reduced perfusion. Rest pain is classically in the forefoot and toes, worse lying flat at night, and relieved by hanging the foot off the bed, because gravity restores a trickle of perfusion.

Pearl — WIfI

The WIfI classification stages limb-threat risk along three axes: Wound, Ischemia (ABI/toe pressure), and foot Infection. It reframes the decision from "is there a pulse" to "what is this specific limb's risk of amputation and likely benefit from revascularization," which is how modern vascular surgeons actually think.

CLTI is limb-threatening and demands revascularization wherever feasible, by bypass or endovascular means, plus aggressive wound care and infection control. The landmark BEST-CLI trial showed that for patients with suitable great saphenous vein, open bypass gave better outcomes than an endovascular-first strategy, reviving the role of surgical bypass in the right patient.

Red flag

Untreated CLTI carries a high one-year risk of major amputation and death. Rest pain, a non-healing wound, or any gangrene is not something to observe; it is a referral for revascularization.

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) Emergency

ALI is the sudden loss of arterial perfusion threatening limb viability, classically within two weeks of onset. It is a true emergency: muscle tolerates roughly 4–6 hours of ischemia before irreversible injury begins.

Mnemonic — the 6 P's

Pain · Pallor · Pulselessness · Paresthesia · Paralysis · Poikilothermia (Perishingly cold)

The order matters clinically: paresthesia and paralysis are the ominous late signs. A limb with sensory or motor loss is on the clock; pain and pallor alone are earlier and more recoverable.

» Embolic vs thrombotic — the central distinction

Determining the mechanism changes the operation, so this comparison is asked relentlessly.

Most common source of an arterial embolus, and most common site it lodges?

The heart is the usual source (most often atrial fibrillation). Emboli lodge at branch points where the vessel narrows; in the leg the common femoral bifurcation is the classic site.

» Management

  1. Anticoagulate immediately. Give a systemic heparin bolus and infusion the moment ALI is suspected, to prevent clot propagation, before any imaging.
  2. Assess viability (Rutherford class). The class decides the path: I and IIa allow time for imaging and possibly thrombolysis; IIb needs the OR now; III is non-salvageable.
  3. Revascularize. Options include catheter embolectomy with a Fogarty balloon (classic for embolic IIb), catheter-directed thrombolysis (often suited to thrombotic disease or category I–IIa), surgical bypass, or endovascular thrombectomy.
  4. Anticipate reperfusion. Restoring flow to ischemic muscle releases potassium, myoglobin, and acid. Watch for hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis, and compartment syndrome, and have a low threshold for fasciotomy.
Red flag — reperfusion injury

After revascularizing a limb ischemic for several hours, a tense, painful compartment with pain on passive stretch is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Pulses may still be present; do not wait for them to disappear. The treatment is urgent four-compartment fasciotomy. Concurrently watch the potassium and the kidneys for rhabdomyolysis.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is a focal dilation greater than 1.5× the normal vessel diameter; for the infrarenal aorta that means a diameter ≥ 3 cm. The overwhelming majority are infrarenal and degenerative (atherosclerotic). Risk factors are smoking (the strongest modifiable one), male sex, older age, family history, and other connective tissue contributors.

Pearl — true vs false aneurysm

A true aneurysm involves all three layers of the wall (intima, media, adventitia). A pseudoaneurysm (false aneurysm) is a contained rupture, blood held in by only the outer layers or surrounding tissue, classically at an anastomosis or a femoral puncture site. The distinction is a frequent pimp.

» Screening & surveillance

The USPSTF recommends a one-time screening ultrasound for men aged 65–75 who have ever smoked. Small aneurysms are followed by serial ultrasound at intervals that shorten as the aneurysm grows.

» When to repair (elective)

  • Diameter ≥ 5.5 cm in men
  • Diameter ≥ 5.0 cm in women
  • Rapid expansion > 0.5 cm in 6 months or > 1 cm in a year
  • Symptomatic (tender aneurysm, distal embolization) at any size
Red flag — ruptured AAA

The classic triad is sudden abdominal or back/flank pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass. A hypotensive older patient with back pain is a ruptured AAA until proven otherwise. If unstable, the patient goes straight to repair — no CT. Practice permissive (hypotensive) resuscitation, keeping systolic pressure only high enough to perfuse the brain (around 70–90 mmHg while awake), because aggressive fluids and a higher pressure can blow out the contained rupture.

Pimp

At what size do you repair an asymptomatic infrarenal AAA in a man?

5.5 cm (5.0 cm in women), or sooner for rapid growth or symptoms. Below threshold, the rupture risk is lower than the operative risk, so you survey and optimize medical therapy.

Other variants to recognize: inflammatory AAA (thickened, inflamed wall, often younger patients, elevated inflammatory markers, classic "ureteral entrapment"), and mycotic (infected) aneurysm (saccular, rapidly expanding, febrile, often Salmonella or Staph).

Carotid disease & stroke prevention

Atherosclerotic plaque at the carotid bifurcation causes stroke by two mechanisms: embolization of plaque/thrombus to the brain (the more common mechanism) and, less often, flow limitation from a tight stenosis. The whole point of carotid intervention is stroke prevention, not symptom relief.

» Symptomatic vs asymptomatic

Symptomatic disease means an ipsilateral neurologic event in the prior 6 months: a TIA, a stroke, or amaurosis fugax (transient monocular blindness, "a curtain coming down," from a retinal-artery embolus). Asymptomatic disease is found on screening or a bruit.

Pimp

What is amaurosis fugax and what does it signify?

Transient, painless monocular vision loss from an embolus to the ophthalmic/retinal artery (a branch of the internal carotid). It is a form of TIA and marks the carotid as symptomatic, lowering the threshold to intervene.

» Indications — what the trials showed

Two landmark trial families set the thresholds. NASCET established benefit of carotid endarterectomy in symptomatic patients; ACAS/ACST did so for asymptomatic patients.

» CEA vs CAS vs TCAR

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) remains the standard. Transfemoral carotid artery stenting (CAS) is an option, particularly for high surgical risk or hostile necks, but the CREST trial showed stenting carried a higher periprocedural stroke risk while surgery carried a higher MI risk, with stroke risk rising in older patients undergoing stenting. Transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) uses direct carotid access with flow reversal for embolic protection and has become a popular middle path.

Red flag — timing after stroke

After a completed stroke, revascularization is generally done within about two weeks in stable patients (to prevent recurrence) but avoided in the hyperacute setting of a large infarct, where reperfusion can cause hemorrhagic transformation. Coordinate with neurology.

Mesenteric ischemia

Reduced blood flow to the gut, splitting into an acute, emergent form and a chronic, indolent one. The arteries at stake are the celiac trunk, the SMA, and the IMA.

» Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI)

The hallmark is pain out of proportion to the physical exam: a patient writhing in severe abdominal pain with a soft, relatively benign-feeling belly early on. Four mechanisms:

Management: resuscitate, anticoagulate with heparin, broad-spectrum antibiotics, urgent revascularization (embolectomy, bypass, or endovascular), and resect frankly necrotic bowel, with a planned second-look laparotomy at 24–48 hours to reassess marginal segments.

Pimp

Why does an SMA embolus spare the proximal jejunum?

The embolus typically lodges a few centimeters into the SMA, distal to the first jejunal branches and the middle colic origin, so the proximal jejunum (and sometimes the proximal colon) retains perfusion while the rest of the small bowel infarcts.

» Chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI)

"Intestinal angina." The triad is postprandial abdominal pain, food fear (sitophobia), and weight loss, in an older arteriopath. Because of the rich collateral network, symptoms usually require significant disease in at least two of the three mesenteric vessels. Treatment is revascularization, increasingly endovascular (angioplasty/stent of the SMA).

Pearl — MALS

Median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS) is extrinsic compression of the celiac artery by the diaphragm's median arcuate ligament, classically in younger, thinner patients with postprandial pain and an epigastric bruit that changes with respiration. Treatment is surgical release of the ligament, not stenting.

Aortic dissection Emergency

A tear in the aortic intima lets blood track into the media, creating a false lumen that can propagate, occlude branch vessels (malperfusion), or rupture. The presentation is sudden, severe "tearing" chest or back pain, sometimes with a pulse or blood-pressure differential between limbs, or signs of organ malperfusion.

» Initial medical management (any type)

The goal is to reduce the shearing force on the aortic wall, dP/dt. Give an IV beta-blocker first (esmolol or labetalol) to bring heart rate and contractility down, then add a vasodilator such as nitroprusside if blood pressure is still high. Target heart rate around 60 and systolic ~100–120.

Red flag — sequence matters

Never give a vasodilator before beta-blockade. A vasodilator alone triggers reflex tachycardia, which raises dP/dt and can propagate the dissection. Beta-blocker first, always.

Type A dissections go to cardiac/vascular surgery emergently. Type B dissections are managed medically unless complicated by malperfusion, rupture, rapid expansion, or refractory pain/hypertension, in which case TEVAR is the usual intervention.

Deep vein thrombosis & VTE

Venous thromboembolism spans DVT and pulmonary embolism. The pathophysiology is captured by Virchow's triad.

Mnemonic — Virchow's triad

Stasis · Endothelial injury · Hypercoagulability

Every DVT risk factor (immobility, surgery, malignancy, OCPs, inherited thrombophilia) maps onto one of these three.

Diagnosis combines pretest probability (the Wells score), D-dimer (useful to rule out when low-risk), and compression duplex ultrasound (the confirmatory test, showing a non-compressible vein). Treatment is anticoagulation; an IVC filter is reserved mainly for patients with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation or recurrent PE despite it.

Red flag — phlegmasia

Massive iliofemoral DVT can cause phlegmasia cerulea dolens: a swollen, blue, painful leg from near-total venous outflow obstruction, which can progress to venous gangrene and compartment syndrome. It is a limb-threatening emergency that may need thrombolysis or thrombectomy, not anticoagulation alone. (Its milder, white-leg precursor is phlegmasia alba dolens.)

Pearl — May-Thurner

May-Thurner syndrome is compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery against the spine, predisposing to left-leg DVT, classically in younger women. It is the reason an "unprovoked" left iliofemoral DVT deserves a look at the iliac vein, often treated with thrombolysis plus venous stenting.

Chronic venous insufficiency & varicose veins

When venous valves fail, blood refluxes and venous pressure stays high (venous hypertension), producing varicosities, leg heaviness, edema, skin changes, and ultimately ulceration. Severity is graded by the CEAP classification.

Mnemonic — CEAP

Clinical · Etiologic · Anatomic · Pathophysiologic

The clinical axis runs C0 (no signs) → C1 (telangiectasia/reticular) → C2 (varicose veins) → C3 (edema) → C4 (skin changes: pigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis) → C5 (healed ulcer) → C6 (active venous ulcer).

Never apply compression to a leg you have not confirmed has adequate arterial inflow. Compressing an ischemic (arterial) leg can precipitate tissue loss. Check the ABI before wrapping a "venous-looking" ulcer.

Management of CVI: compression therapy and leg elevation are first-line; definitive options for refluxing superficial veins include endovenous thermal ablation (radiofrequency or laser), sclerotherapy, and phlebectomy.

The diabetic foot

Diabetic foot disease is the convergence of three processes: peripheral neuropathy (loss of protective sensation → unnoticed trauma), ischemia (PAD, often tibial/below-knee with sparing of the foot vessels), and infection. Each worsens the others, and together they make the diabetic foot the leading cause of non-traumatic amputation.

  • Neuropathic ulcers classically sit under pressure points (metatarsal heads, heel) in a foot with intact but unfelt circulation.
  • Charcot foot is progressive neuropathic joint destruction producing the collapsed "rocker-bottom" deformity; in the acute phase it can mimic infection (red, hot, swollen) without a wound.
  • Severity is staged with systems such as Wagner and the University of Texas classifications.
Pearl

Management is multidisciplinary and rests on the same logic as the rest of the service: offload pressure, debride and control infection, and revascularize if there is ischemia. A "probe-to-bone" positive ulcer suggests osteomyelitis. Because diabetic disease favors the tibial vessels with patent pedal targets, distal bypass or pedal-loop angioplasty can salvage feet that look unsalvageable.

Other key presentations

Briefer, but each is a known consult and a known pimp.

Popliteal aneurysm
Clots, not ruptures

Most common peripheral aneurysm. The danger is thrombosis and distal embolization (limb loss), not rupture. Frequently bilateral and associated with AAA — so check the other leg and the aorta.

Thoracic outlet
Three flavors

Neurogenic (most common, brachial plexus). Venous = Paget-Schroetter "effort thrombosis" of the subclavian vein. Arterial (rarest) from a cervical rib. Decompression often means first-rib resection.

Subclavian steal
Arm steals from brain

Proximal subclavian stenosis → retrograde flow down the vertebral artery to feed the arm, causing posterior-circulation symptoms with arm exertion. More common on the left.

Renal artery stenosis
Two populations

Atherosclerotic (older, ostial) and fibromuscular dysplasia (younger women, "string of beads" mid-vessel). Causes renovascular hypertension and flash pulmonary edema.

Splenic a. aneurysm
Most common visceral

The commonest splanchnic artery aneurysm; notable risk of rupture in pregnancy and a female predominance. Treat at smaller sizes in women of childbearing age.

Buerger's disease
Young smoker, digits

Thromboangiitis obliterans: non-atherosclerotic inflammatory occlusion of small/medium vessels in young male smokers, causing digital ischemia. The only real treatment is absolute smoking cessation.

Popliteal entrapment
Young athlete, no risk factors

Abnormal relationship of the popliteal artery to the gastrocnemius compresses flow with exertion. Suspect claudication in a fit young person with normal arteries at rest.

Vascular trauma
Hard vs soft signs

Hard signs (pulsatile bleeding, expanding hematoma, bruit/thrill, absent pulse, ischemia) → OR. Soft signs → imaging (CTA) and observation.

Large-vessel vasculitis
Two named patterns

Takayasu ("pulseless disease," young Asian women, aortic-arch branches). Giant cell arteritis (>50, temporal artery, jaw claudication, vision-threatening).

SECTION 03

Major operations

The cases you'll scrub. For each: the indication, the key steps, the anatomy you'll be asked to name, and the complications to anticipate.

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA)

Indication: stroke prevention in significant carotid stenosis (see the carotid section for thresholds). Concept: open the carotid and peel out the atherosclerotic plaque, then close, usually with a patch.

» Key steps

  1. Position & expose. Head turned away; longitudinal incision along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid. The facial vein is a reliable landmark overlying the bifurcation and is divided.
  2. Identify and protect the nerves. Find the vagus (posterolateral in the sheath), the hypoglossal (CN XII) crossing the vessels superiorly, and the ansa cervicalis descending from it.
  3. Heparinize, then clamp. Give systemic heparin, wait for the ACT, then clamp the ICA, CCA, and ECA. Decide on a shunt based on neuromonitoring or stump pressure.
  4. Arteriotomy & endarterectomy. Open the artery and develop the plane, removing the plaque with a smooth tapered endpoint in the ICA.
  5. Close with a patch. A patch angioplasty (vein or prosthetic) widens the lumen and lowers restenosis vs primary closure. Restore flow to the ECA first, then the ICA, so any debris flushes to the face, not the brain.
Pimp

Which cranial nerves are at risk in a CEA, and which is most commonly injured?

At risk: hypoglossal (XII) → tongue deviates toward the lesion; vagus (X) / recurrent laryngeal → hoarseness; marginal mandibular branch of the facial (VII) → drooping corner of the mouth; and glossopharyngeal (IX) with very high exposure → dysphagia. The marginal mandibular branch and the hypoglossal are the most frequently injured.

Red flag — two postoperative emergencies

Expanding neck hematoma can compress the airway within minutes; it is a surgical airway emergency — open the incision at the bedside if the airway is threatened. Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome (ipsilateral headache, seizures, possible hemorrhage days after reperfusion of a chronically hypoperfused hemisphere) is driven by uncontrolled hypertension — manage the blood pressure aggressively.

Open AAA repair

Indication: AAA meeting size/growth/symptom thresholds, especially when anatomy is unfavorable for EVAR, or rupture. Concept: clamp the aorta, open the sac, and sew in a graft (tube or bifurcated).

» Key steps

  1. Approach. Transperitoneal (midline) or retroperitoneal (left flank, better for hostile abdomens and high/juxtarenal necks).
  2. Proximal & distal control. Expose the infrarenal neck and the iliacs; heparinize.
  3. Clamp & open. Clamp proximally (infrarenal if possible) and distally, open the aneurysm sac, and oversew back-bleeding lumbar arteries and the IMA from inside.
  4. Graft. Sew a tube graft if the iliacs are spared, or a bifurcated graft if they are aneurysmal, then close the aneurysm sac over the graft.
  5. Reperfuse carefully. Release the clamp slowly, coordinating with anesthesia for declamping hypotension.
Red flag — the morbid complications

Beyond MI (the leading cause of death), watch for: colonic (sigmoid) ischemia from IMA sacrifice — bloody diarrhea in the first days post-op demands flexible sigmoidoscopy; renal failure from suprarenal clamping or embolization; spinal cord ischemia; declamping hypotension; and, late, graft infection and aortoenteric fistula (herald GI bleed years later where graft erodes into the duodenum).

Pimp

Post-op day 2 after open AAA: bloody diarrhea. What happened and what do you do?

Sigmoid colonic ischemia from interruption of IMA flow without adequate collateral (marginal artery of Drummond). Resuscitate and get an urgent flexible sigmoidoscopy; transmural necrosis means a return to the OR for resection.

EVAR & endoleaks Pimp-heavy

Concept: endovascular aneurysm repair excludes the sac from the inside using a stent-graft delivered through the femoral arteries, so blood flows through the graft while the aneurysm sac thromboses. It is less invasive than open repair with a faster recovery, at the cost of lifelong surveillance and a risk of endoleak.

Suitability depends on anatomy: an adequate proximal neck (length, diameter, limited angulation and thrombus) for a seal, and iliofemoral access vessels large and straight enough to deliver the device.

Most common endoleak, and which types you must fix?

Type II (lumbar/IMA backflow) is most common and is usually watched if the sac is stable. Types I and III are high-pressure leaks that pressurize the sac and carry rupture risk, so they are repaired.

Pearl

"Endoleak" (blood still in the sac) is different from "endotension" (sac pressurized/enlarging without a demonstrable leak, = type V) and from "post-implantation syndrome" (a benign self-limited fever/leukocytosis after EVAR). Lifelong CT or duplex surveillance is mandatory because leaks and sac growth can appear years later.

Lower extremity bypass

Indication: CLTI, or disabling claudication failing medical therapy, with a pattern not suited to endovascular treatment. Concept: route blood around an occluded segment from a healthy inflow vessel to a patent outflow (target) vessel, using a conduit.

Common configurations: femoral–popliteal (above- or below-knee) and femoral–distal/tibial for more distal disease.

Pimp

Best conduit for an infrainguinal (especially below-knee) bypass?

Autologous great saphenous vein. Vein outperforms prosthetic (PTFE/Dacron) for patency below the knee. Prosthetic is acceptable for above-knee fem-pop when no vein is available, but vein is always preferred for distal targets.

  • Reversed vs in-situ vein: reversed flips the vein so its valves point with flow; in-situ leaves it in place and lyses the valves with a valvulotome (and ligates side branches).
  • Inflow/outflow must both be sound: a bypass fails if you graft onto a poor target or from a stenotic inflow.
  • Grafts get duplex surveillance — a "failing" graft (rising velocities at a stenosis) is fixed before it thromboses, because a failed (clotted) graft is far harder to salvage.
Red flag

Early graft thrombosis (within 30 days) is usually a technical problem (kink, anastomotic stenosis, poor target). Late thrombosis is usually intimal hyperplasia or progression of disease. Either way, the limb may go acutely ischemic, so a clotted bypass is an urgent problem.

Peripheral endovascular intervention

The endovascular toolkit treats stenoses and occlusions from inside the vessel, via percutaneous access (antegrade or retrograde common femoral, or contralateral "up-and-over").

PTA
Balloon angioplasty

Dilates the lesion. Plain or, increasingly, drug-coated (paclitaxel) to reduce restenosis.

Stenting
Scaffold the vessel

Self-expanding nitinol stents for flexible/long segments (SFA); balloon-expandable for precise, high-radial-force spots (ostia, iliacs).

Atherectomy

Removes or modifies plaque (e.g., calcified lesions) before angioplasty.

Thrombolysis
Dissolve fresh clot

Catheter-directed tPA for acute thrombotic occlusion — favored for ALI categories I–IIa.

Dialysis access & AV fistula

Concept: create a high-flow connection between an artery and a vein so the vein "arterializes," enlarges, and can be cannulated for hemodialysis. The guiding principle is "fistula first", autologous fistula over graft over catheter, and distal before proximal to preserve future sites.

Steal syndrome: the fistula diverts so much flow that the hand distal to it becomes ischemic (pain, pallor, paresthesia with dialysis). Also watch for failure to mature, aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm, central venous stenosis, thrombosis, and high-output cardiac failure from very high flow.

Amputations

Indication: non-salvageable ischemia or infection, intractable rest pain in a non-reconstructable limb, or life-threatening sepsis from a necrotic foot. The art is choosing the most distal level that will heal while preserving function.

Why try for a BKA over an AKA, and when do you do a guillotine first?

Preserving the knee greatly increases the odds of walking with a prosthesis and lowers the metabolic cost of gait. A guillotine amputation is a first-stage, open amputation to control ascending infection in a septic patient; you return for definitive closure once the patient is stable and the infection controlled.

Embolectomy / thrombectomy

Indication: acute limb ischemia, classically an embolic occlusion (category IIb). Concept: a Fogarty balloon catheter is passed beyond the clot through an arteriotomy, the balloon inflated, and the catheter withdrawn to drag the thrombus/embolus out, repeated until inflow and backflow are restored. Completion angiography confirms a clean vessel; anticipate reperfusion injury and the need for fasciotomy.

TEVAR (thoracic endovascular aortic repair)

Indications: descending thoracic aortic aneurysm, complicated type B dissection, and blunt traumatic aortic injury (transection, classically at the isthmus just distal to the left subclavian). Concept: a covered stent-graft excludes the diseased segment via femoral access.

Red flag — spinal cord ischemia

Covering long segments of thoracic aorta can occlude intercostal arteries feeding the spinal cord (the artery of Adamkiewicz), risking paraplegia. Mitigation includes CSF (lumbar) drainage to lower spinal canal pressure and maintaining higher mean arterial pressure to support cord perfusion. Coverage of the left subclavian may require revascularization to protect the arm, posterior brain, and cord.

Fasciotomy

Indication: compartment syndrome, most often after revascularization of an acutely ischemic limb (reperfusion edema), or after trauma. Concept: release the fascia of all compartments to relieve pressure and restore perfusion before muscle and nerve die.

Mnemonic — four compartments of the lower leg

Anterior · Lateral · Superficial posterior · Deep posterior

The standard two-incision technique releases all four. The anterior compartment is most often involved; injury to the deep peroneal nerve there causes foot drop and numbness in the first web space.

Red flag

The earliest and most reliable sign of compartment syndrome is pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch of the compartment's muscles. Pulselessness is a late sign. If you wait for the pulse to disappear, you have waited too long. When in doubt, measure compartment pressures or simply decompress.

Aortobifemoral bypass & others

Aortobifemoral (ABF) bypass is the durable open answer to extensive aortoiliac occlusive disease (Leriche), routing a bifurcated graft from the infrarenal aorta to both common femoral arteries; for unilateral disease or poor open candidates, axillobifemoral or femorofemoral "extra-anatomic" bypasses are alternatives. Other named operations you'll hear: femoral endarterectomy/profundaplasty (open clean-out of the CFA/profunda, often combined with inflow or outflow procedures), thoracic outlet decompression via first-rib resection, and IVC filter placement for VTE with contraindication to anticoagulation.

Pimp

What is the most common cause of late death after AAA repair (and vascular operations generally)?

Myocardial infarction. These patients have diffuse atherosclerosis; the operated territory may be fixed, but the coronaries remain the leading threat to life, which is why cardiac optimization is central to vascular care.

SECTION 04

Getting pimped

The rapid-fire questions, grouped by the angle they come from. Read the question, answer it out loud before you read the answer, and move on. These complement the questions seeded through the earlier sections rather than repeating them.

Anatomy questions

Pimp

Name the branches of the abdominal aorta from the diaphragm down.

In order: inferior phrenics, celiac trunk (T12), SMA (L1), middle suprarenals, renals (L1–L2), gonadals, IMA (L3), lumbars, median sacral, then the bifurcation into the common iliacs at L4.

Pimp

What supplies the colon, and where are the watershed areas?

The SMA (ileocolic, right colic, middle colic) supplies the right and proximal transverse colon; the IMA (left colic, sigmoids, superior rectal) supplies the distal transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon. The watersheds between these territories are Griffith's point at the splenic flexure and Sudeck's point at the rectosigmoid junction, which is why those segments are the most vulnerable to ischemic colitis after aortic surgery or low-flow states.

Pimp

What are the borders and contents of the femoral triangle?

Borders: inguinal ligament superiorly, sartorius laterally, adductor longus medially. Contents from lateral to medial follow the mnemonic NAVEL: femoral Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space (femoral canal), Lymphatics. The nerve sits outside the femoral sheath; the artery is your access point and the vein your landmark for central access.

Pimp

What is in the carotid sheath, and how are the structures arranged?

The common/internal carotid artery (medial), the internal jugular vein (lateral), and the vagus nerve (posterior, between and behind the two vessels). The ansa cervicalis lies on the surface of the sheath and the hypoglossal crosses superiorly.

Pimp

What are the layers of the arterial wall, and which one do you take in an endarterectomy?

From inside out: intima (endothelium), media (smooth muscle and elastin), and adventitia (collagen, vasa vasorum). Endarterectomy develops a plane in the media, lifting the plaque with the intima and leaving outer media and adventitia behind.

Pimp

Where does the GSV run, and where does it join the deep system?

The great saphenous vein runs from the medial dorsal foot, anterior to the medial malleolus (a reliable cutdown landmark), up the medial leg and thigh to the saphenofemoral junction, where it joins the common femoral vein at the fossa ovalis. Its consistent course and caliber make it the conduit of choice for bypass.

Physiology & hemodynamics

Pimp

Why does flow fall off so steeply as a vessel narrows?

Because of Poiseuille's law, flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius. Halving the radius cuts flow roughly sixteen-fold, which is why a stenosis becomes hemodynamically significant only once it is fairly tight (about a 50% diameter / 75% area reduction) and why small gains in luminal diameter from angioplasty translate into large gains in flow.

Pimp

Use Laplace's law to explain why big aneurysms rupture.

Wall tension is proportional to pressure times radius (for a given wall thickness). As the aneurysm enlarges, the radius rises, wall tension climbs, the wall thins, and the process feeds forward, so rupture risk rises sharply with diameter. This is the mechanical basis for size-based repair thresholds.

Pimp

What is reperfusion injury and what is its systemic danger?

Restoring flow to ischemic muscle releases potassium, lactate, myoglobin, and reactive oxygen species into the circulation. The systemic threats are hyperkalemia and arrhythmia, metabolic acidosis, and myoglobinuric acute kidney injury; locally the swelling drives compartment syndrome. Anticipate it after revascularizing any significantly ischemic limb.

Clinical & operative

Pimp

A patient is six hours out from a femoral embolectomy with a tense, painful calf and pain on passive dorsiflexion. What is going on and what do you do?

Compartment syndrome from reperfusion. The decision is clinical, not contingent on a pressure number or a pulse — take the patient for a four-compartment fasciotomy. Waiting for pulselessness means waiting for dead muscle.

Pimp

After open infrarenal AAA repair a patient passes bloody stool on day two. What is the concern and the first test?

Ischemic colitis of the sigmoid from interrupted IMA flow with inadequate collateral supply. The first step is flexible sigmoidoscopy at the bedside to assess mucosal viability; transmural necrosis mandates resection.

Pimp

Why give heparin before you clamp, and how do you reverse it?

Clamping creates a column of stagnant blood that will thrombose; systemic heparin (about 80–100 U/kg, confirmed by an ACT above ~250) keeps that blood and the runoff bed from clotting. Reverse with protamine, roughly 1 mg per 100 units of heparin, watching for hypotension and the protamine reaction.

Pimp

A week after CEA the patient has a severe ipsilateral headache and a seizure. Diagnosis?

Cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome — a chronically hypoperfused hemisphere loses autoregulation and is suddenly exposed to normal pressure. The treatment is aggressive blood-pressure control; untreated it can progress to intracerebral hemorrhage.

Pimp

Which endoleak is most common, and which ones force you to reintervene?

A type II endoleak (retrograde filling from a branch such as the IMA or a lumbar) is most common and usually observed unless the sac grows. Type I (seal-zone) and type III (component separation or graft defect) pressurize the sac directly and must be repaired.

Pimp

When does an asymptomatic AAA get fixed?

At 5.5 cm in men and about 5.0 cm in women, or for rapid growth (more than ~0.5 cm in six months / 1 cm in a year), or once it becomes symptomatic or tender. Below those thresholds the rupture risk is lower than the operative risk, so you survey with ultrasound.

Classic one-liners

Pearl — the ones that come up again and again
  • Leriche syndrome triad → claudication (buttock/thigh), absent femoral pulses, erectile dysfunction.
  • Most common source of an arterial embolus → the heart, usually atrial fibrillation.
  • Most common site an embolus lodges in the leg → the common femoral bifurcation.
  • Conduit of choice for bypass → the patient's own great saphenous vein.
  • Single most effective risk-factor modification in PAD → smoking cessation.
  • Most common cause of late death after vascular surgery → myocardial infarction.
  • Subclavian steal → proximal subclavian stenosis, far more often on the left, reversing vertebral flow.
  • Pain out of proportion to exam → acute mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise.
  • The three mesenteric vessels → celiac, SMA, IMA; chronic mesenteric ischemia needs at least two significantly diseased.
  • Cold, pale, pulseless leg with the 6 Ps → acute limb ischemia; heparinize first.
  • Tearing chest/back pain with a pulse or pressure differential → aortic dissection; control the heart rate before the pressure.
  • Tongue deviates toward the side of injury → hypoglossal (CN XII), the classic CEA nerve.
SECTION 05

Rotation survival

The unwritten curriculum: how to sound like you belong, how to behave in the room, and how to work up the consults that land on the vascular service every day.

Presenting a vascular patient

A vascular presentation is organized around a vessel and the tissue it feeds, so lead with the vascular bed and the severity of the threat to it, then build the rest of the story around that. The attending wants to know how worried to be and how quickly, which means your first two sentences should locate the problem and grade it.

  1. One-line summary. Age, relevant risk factors (smoking, diabetes, prior revascularization), and the vascular problem framed by severity — for example, “68-year-old smoker with rest pain and a non-healing toe ulcer, so tissue-loss CLTI,” not simply “leg pain.”
  2. Symptom in vascular terms. For arterial disease, distinguish claudication from rest pain from tissue loss; for an aneurysm, whether it is asymptomatic, symptomatic, or ruptured; for carotid disease, whether it is asymptomatic or symptomatic and when the event was.
  3. The pulse and perfusion exam. Report pulses at each level (femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial) graded consistently, the ABI or toe pressures, capillary refill, skin and ulcer description, and the contralateral side for comparison. This is the heart of the exam and should be specific.
  4. Hemodynamic and cardiac status. Because the vascular patient is a cardiac patient, note blood pressure, heart rate and rhythm (atrial fibrillation matters), and any active cardiac, pulmonary, or renal issues bearing on operative risk.
  5. Imaging and the question. Summarize the duplex, CTA, or angiogram in terms of inflow, outflow, and target, then close with a clear assessment and a proposed plan rather than trailing off. Ending on a recommendation, even a tentative one, is what distinguishes a strong presentation.
Pearl

When you describe any revascularization candidate, think and speak in the language of inflow, outflow, and conduit. Naming the inflow source, the outflow target, and the conduit you would use signals that you understand the operation, not just the diagnosis.

In the operating room

Your job as a student or junior is to be useful, unobtrusive, and anticipatory, which mostly means protecting the field, following the flow of the case, and being ready half a step before you are asked. The fastest way to earn more responsibility is to demonstrate that you are paying attention to the operation rather than to the clock.

  • Scrub and glove correctly, and stay sterile. Keep your hands above the waist and in front of you, do not turn your back to the field, and tell someone immediately if you think you have contaminated rather than hoping no one noticed.
  • Hold retraction steadily and without complaint. Exposure is the operation; a still, well-placed retractor that gives the surgeon a view of the anastomosis is a genuine contribution. Maintain the tension you were given until you are asked to change it.
  • Follow the rhythm of the vascular case. Expect the sequence of expose, control proximal and distal, heparinize, clamp, perform the anastomosis or endarterectomy, flush, unclamp, and confirm flow, and you will understand why each instrument is being asked for.
  • Anticipate heparin, clamps, and flushing. Know that systemic heparin precedes clamping, that flow is restored in an order that flushes debris away from the brain or distal bed, and that the team will check an ACT, so these moments will not surprise you.
  • Know the anatomy of the case you are in. Read the operation the night before and be able to name the structures in the wound; this is precisely where the pimping in Section 04 comes from, and answering in the room is far more impressive than answering in the workroom.
  • Manage the small things. Learn to tie and to cut suture at the length the surgeon prefers, keep the field dry with suction when asked, and handle tissue gently, since rough handling of an artery or a vein graft has real consequences.

Working up the common consults

A handful of consults make up most of the pages to a vascular service, and each has a predictable workup, so knowing the first few moves lets you start the evaluation before the senior arrives. In every case the unifying questions are how acute the problem is, how threatened the tissue is, and what the perfusion exam and the imaging show.

» The acutely cold, painful leg

Treat this as acute limb ischemia until proven otherwise, which makes it the most time-sensitive vascular consult. Run the 6 Ps, establish onset and whether the patient is in atrial fibrillation or has prior claudication, examine and Doppler both legs, and start systemic heparin immediately unless contraindicated. Then grade the limb with the Rutherford acute-ischemia categories to decide whether you have time for imaging and catheter-directed therapy or whether the threat (a IIb limb) demands the operating room now.

» The non-healing foot ulcer

Frame this around the triad of neuropathy, ischemia, and infection, since the management hinges on which are present. Examine pulses and obtain an ABI and toe pressures (remembering that diabetic vessels can be falsely noncompressible), probe the wound and assess for deep infection or osteomyelitis, and look for the rocker-bottom deformity of Charcot. The plan follows three threads in parallel — offload and debride, treat infection, and revascularize if perfusion is inadequate — and tibial disease with patent pedal targets is the classic setting for distal bypass or pedal intervention.

» The asymptomatic AAA found on imaging

The two questions are how big and is it symptomatic. Confirm the diameter, ask specifically about back or abdominal pain and tenderness that would reclassify it as symptomatic, and apply the repair thresholds (about 5.5 cm in men, 5.0 cm in women, rapid growth, or symptoms). Below threshold the work is surveillance ultrasound and risk-factor control, principally smoking cessation and cardiovascular optimization, while you arrange anatomy-defining CTA only when repair is on the table.

» The carotid bruit or the asymptomatic stenosis

Decide first whether the patient is symptomatic, meaning a stroke, TIA, or amaurosis fugax in the matching territory, because symptomatic disease is treated more aggressively and more urgently. Get a carotid duplex to grade the stenosis, ensure the patient is on optimal medical therapy (antiplatelet, statin, blood-pressure control, smoking cessation) regardless of what else is decided, and then apply the thresholds — symptomatic stenosis at or above 50–70% generally warrants intervention, asymptomatic at higher grades is more selective — with the timing for symptomatic disease usually within about two weeks of the event.

Red flag — the consults you do not sit on

A IIb acutely ischemic limb, a ruptured or symptomatic AAA, acute mesenteric ischemia with pain out of proportion, and an acute type A dissection are emergencies. For these the correct move is to start resuscitation and heparin or rate control as appropriate and to call the senior or attending at once, not to complete a leisurely workup.

Red flag

Never apply compression to a leg with significant arterial disease. Confirm an adequate ABI before compressing a venous ulcer, because compressing an ischemic limb can precipitate tissue loss.

Numbers worth memorizing

These are the figures attendings expect on demand. Commit them as flat facts; the reasoning lives in the sections above.

AAA — definition
≥ 3 cm

Or 1.5× the expected normal aortic diameter. Infrarenal is the usual location.

AAA — repair threshold
5.5 cm (men) · 5.0 cm (women)

Also repair for rapid growth (> 0.5 cm/6 mo or 1 cm/yr) or symptoms.

AAA — screening
Men 65–75 who ever smoked

One-time duplex ultrasound (USPSTF).

Carotid — symptomatic
≥ 50–70%

Intervene; benefit greatest at ≥ 70%. Timing usually within ~2 weeks of the event.

Carotid — asymptomatic
≥ 60–70%

More selective; requires good life expectancy and optimal medical therapy.

Critical stenosis
~50% diameter / ~75% area

The point at which a lesion becomes hemodynamically significant.

Intraoperative heparin
80–100 U/kg · ACT > 250

Reverse with protamine ~1 mg per 100 U heparin.

Muscle ischemia window
~4–6 hours

Skeletal muscle tolerates only a few hours of warm ischemia — time is tissue.

Fistula — Rule of 6s
6 wk · >600 mL/min · >6 mm · <6 mm deep

Maturity targets for a usable arteriovenous fistula.

Aortic branch levels
Celiac T12 · SMA L1 · renals L1–2 · IMA L3 · bifurcation L4

The vertebral landmarks for the abdominal aorta.

Chronic mesenteric ischemia
≥ 2 of 3 vessels

Symptoms need significant disease in at least two of celiac, SMA, IMA.

Poiseuille
Flow ∝ radius⁴

Why tight stenoses matter and why small diameter gains help so much.

How to use this

This guide is a study and orientation aid for trainees rotating on vascular surgery. It distills foundational anatomy, the major presentations and operations, and the questions you are most likely to be asked. It is deliberately concise and is not a clinical protocol. Thresholds, classifications, and management evolve, so verify specifics against current Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) and European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) guidelines and your institution's practice before applying them to a patient. Read it the night before, skim it on the way in, and let the cases turn the lists into knowledge.

Inflow · Outflow · Conduit — and the vascular patient is always a cardiac patient.
