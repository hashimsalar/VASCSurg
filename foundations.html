Arterial anatomy, head to toe Memorize

You will be asked to name vessels in order, in the operating room, while holding a retractor. Learn the path of blood from the aorta to the toes and from the arch to the fingertips.

» The aorta and its branches

The thoracic aorta gives the coronary arteries, then the three arch vessels, then descends. The classic arch order, right to left, is the brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk, the left common carotid, and the left subclavian. The brachiocephalic then splits into the right common carotid and right subclavian.

Below the diaphragm the abdominal aorta gives, in descending order, the celiac trunk (roughly T12), the superior mesenteric artery (L1), the paired renal arteries (L1 to L2), the inferior mesenteric artery (L3), and finally bifurcates into the common iliac arteries at about L4.

Mnemonic

Arch order, right to left, is the "ABCs of the arch": Arteria innominata (brachiocephalic), left common Carotid, left Subclavian. And the celiac trunk's three branches are the left gastric, splenic, and common hepatic.

» The lower extremity, in order

This is the single most asked sequence in the field. Trace it until it is automatic.

  • The common iliac splits into the internal iliac (hypogastric), supplying the pelvis, and the external iliac.
  • The external iliac passes under the inguinal ligament and becomes the common femoral artery.
  • The common femoral bifurcates into the profunda femoris (deep femoral), the key collateral vessel of the thigh, and the superficial femoral artery.
  • The superficial femoral travels down the thigh through the adductor (Hunter's) canal and, at the adductor hiatus, becomes the popliteal artery.
  • Below the knee the popliteal gives the anterior tibial artery (which becomes the dorsalis pedis on the foot) and then the tibioperoneal trunk, which splits into the posterior tibial (passing behind the medial malleolus) and the peroneal (fibular) artery.
Question

What are the three tibial ("trifurcation") vessels, and which two feed the pedal arch?

Anterior tibial, posterior tibial, and peroneal. The dorsalis pedis (continuation of the anterior tibial) and the posterior tibial form the pedal arch. The peroneal ends above the ankle and communicates with both but does not normally reach the foot directly.

Question

What runs with the artery in the adductor canal?

The femoral artery and vein, the saphenous nerve, and the nerve to vastus medialis. The saphenous nerve is the named structure they are fishing for.

» The upper extremity, in order

The subclavian passes under the clavicle and over the first rib to become the axillary, then past teres major becomes the brachial, which bifurcates at the elbow into the radial and ulnar arteries. These join in the hand as the superficial and deep palmar arches. This dual supply is what makes the Allen test meaningful and what lets a radiocephalic fistula work.

Venous anatomy

Veins are divided into a deep system (paired with the named arteries, carrying roughly 85 to 90 percent of return), a superficial system, and the perforators that connect them. The two superficial veins you must know are the great saphenous vein, running up the medial leg and thigh to join the common femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction, and the small saphenous vein, running up the posterior calf to the popliteal vein.

Pearl

The great saphenous vein is the workhorse conduit of vascular and cardiac surgery, the preferred graft for infrainguinal bypass and for coronary bypass. Before you ablate or harvest it, someone always asks whether the patient might need it as a conduit later. A vein mapped and spared today can be a limb saved tomorrow.

Collateral pathways High yield

Collaterals explain why a patient can occlude a major vessel and still keep a viable limb or gut. Named collateral routes are favorite teaching material.

Circle of Willis
Anastomotic ring at the base of the brain linking the anterior (carotid) and posterior (vertebrobasilar) circulations. It explains why some patients tolerate carotid occlusion and others stroke.
Marginal artery of Drummond
Continuous arcade along the mesenteric border of the colon connecting the SMA and IMA territories.
Arc of Riolan
A shorter, more central SMA to IMA connection (the "meandering mesenteric artery") that enlarges dramatically when one mesenteric vessel occludes chronically.
Profunda femoris
The main collateral around an occluded superficial femoral artery; geniculate branches reconstitute the popliteal. A good profunda can carry a limb despite a dead SFA.
Aortoiliac collaterals
In Leriche-type occlusion, flow reaches the legs via lumbar to iliolumbar routes and via the IMA to superior rectal to middle and inferior rectal (internal iliac) routes.

Hemodynamics & physiology

A handful of physical principles get asked again and again because they explain disease.

» Poiseuille's law

Flow is proportional to the fourth power of the radius and inversely proportional to length and viscosity. The punchline is that small changes in lumen diameter cause large changes in flow, which is why a focal stenosis can be so hemodynamically costly, and why a slightly larger conduit dramatically improves flow.

» Critical stenosis

A stenosis generally becomes hemodynamically significant once it narrows the diameter by about 50 percent (roughly 75 percent cross-sectional area), at which point a measurable pressure and flow gradient appears across it. This is the threshold that duplex velocity criteria are designed to detect.

» Laplace's law and aneurysms

Wall tension rises with both pressure and radius. As an aneurysm enlarges, wall tension climbs, which raises rupture risk and accelerates further dilation, a self-reinforcing loop that is the entire rationale for size-based repair thresholds.

Question

Why does aneurysm rupture risk rise so steeply with diameter?

By Laplace's law, tension in the wall is proportional to pressure times radius, so a larger radius means higher wall stress at the same blood pressure, which both predisposes to rupture and drives continued expansion.

The vascular history & physical exam

The exam is where you earn credibility. Do it the same way every time.

» History: localizing claudication

The level of arterial disease predicts where the patient hurts when walking, because the pain occurs one level below the occlusion. Buttock and hip claudication points to aortoiliac disease, thigh claudication to the common femoral or iliac level, and the most common pattern, calf claudication, to the superficial femoral and popliteal segment.

Mnemonic · Leriche syndrome

Aortoiliac occlusive disease gives the triad of buttock and thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction, and absent femoral pulses. Picture the blockage sitting at the aortic bifurcation, starving everything downstream.

» The pulse exam

Palpate and grade every pulse, comparing sides. A common scale is 0 absent, 1+ diminished, 2+ normal, 3+ bounding (some services add a fourth grade). Document the sites: radial, brachial, carotid, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, and posterior tibial. If you cannot feel a distal pulse, reach for the handheld Doppler and describe the signal as triphasic, biphasic, or monophasic.

Pearl

"Doppler signals" and "palpable pulses" are not the same thing, and saying so precisely matters. A monophasic Doppler signal in a foot that has no palpable pulse still tells you the foot is perfused, which can be the difference between watchful waiting and an emergent trip to the angiography suite. Always state both what you felt and what you heard.

» Provocative and inspection findings

  • Buerger's test: elevation causes pallor in an ischemic limb, and dependency causes a dusky dependent rubor. The angle at which pallor appears tracks severity.
  • Capillary refill beyond 2 to 3 seconds, along with loss of hair, shiny atrophic skin, and thickened nails, suggests chronic ischemia.
  • Allen test before radial access or radial-artery harvest confirms that the ulnar artery and palmar arch can supply the hand alone.
  • Auscultate for bruits over the carotids, abdomen, and femorals; a bruit signals turbulent flow across a stenosis.

Noninvasive testing & the ABI Core skill

The ankle-brachial index is the bedside number that quantifies perfusion. Measure the higher of the two ankle systolic pressures (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) in each leg and divide by the higher of the two brachial pressures.

Red flag · the falsely reassuring ABI

A normal or high ABI in a diabetic or dialysis patient may be an artifact of medial calcinosis making the vessels incompressible. If the ABI is above 1.40 or does not match a clearly ischemic foot, get a toe-brachial index, since the small digital arteries are usually spared from calcification. A toe-brachial index below 0.70 is abnormal, and a toe pressure below 30 mmHg predicts poor wound healing.

» The rest of the noninvasive toolbox

  • Segmental limb pressures and pulse-volume recordings: cuffs at the thigh, calf, and ankle localize the level of disease by where the pressure drops or the waveform flattens.
  • Exercise ABI: a normal resting ABI that falls after treadmill walking unmasks significant disease and confirms that leg symptoms are vascular.
  • Arterial duplex ultrasound: combines B-mode imaging with Doppler velocities, and a rise in peak systolic velocity across a segment grades the stenosis. This is the first-line tool for surveillance.

Imaging modalities

Duplex US
First-line, no contrast

Cheap, repeatable, operator-dependent. The workhorse for carotid disease, graft surveillance, aneurysm screening and follow-up, and vein mapping.

CTA
The anatomy roadmap

Fast and detailed, the go-to for aneurysm sizing, dissection, trauma, and operative planning. The costs are iodinated contrast and radiation.

MRA
Contrast-sparing option

Good soft-tissue detail without iodinated contrast or radiation. Slower, tends to overestimate stenosis, and raises gadolinium concerns in renal failure.

DSA
The gold standard

Catheter angiography has the highest spatial resolution and is the only modality that is also therapeutic. Invasive, so it is reserved for when intervention is likely or other imaging is equivocal.

Question

What is the gold-standard imaging test for the arterial tree, and why is it not first-line?

Catheter-based digital subtraction angiography. It is invasive and carries access-site, contrast, and radiation risk, so noninvasive duplex and CTA come first; angiography is chosen when you intend to treat in the same setting.

» Intraoperative heparin

Before clamping any artery, the surgeon gives systemic heparin, classically about 80 to 100 units per kg, then confirms an activated clotting time above roughly 250 seconds before applying the clamp. At the end, heparin may be reversed with protamine.

Red flag · protamine reaction and HIT

Protamine, derived from fish sperm, can cause hypotension, bradycardia, and anaphylaxis, especially in patients with prior protamine exposure, fish allergy, or NPH-insulin use, so give it slowly. And never forget heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, an immune reaction (antibodies to the PF4 and heparin complex) appearing about 5 to 10 days after exposure, causing a falling platelet count and paradoxical thrombosis. Stop all heparin, do not transfuse platelets, and anticoagulate with a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban or bivalirudin). Use the 4T score to gauge probability.

Question

A heparinized patient's platelets drop on day 7 and they clot a graft. Diagnosis and next step?

HIT. Stop every source of heparin (including flushes and coated catheters), start a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban, send the HIT antibody and serotonin-release assay, and do not give platelet transfusions, which can worsen thrombosis.

Medical & risk-factor therapy

Revascularization treats the plumbing; medical therapy treats the disease. Every PAD patient should be on best medical therapy regardless of whether they get an operation.

  • Smoking cessation is the single highest-impact intervention, slowing progression and improving graft patency more than any drug.
  • High-intensity statin for every atherosclerotic patient, for plaque stabilization and cardiovascular risk reduction, not just lipids.
  • Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel); low-dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin is now used in selected PAD patients to reduce limb and cardiovascular events.
  • Blood pressure and diabetes control, and an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agent where indicated for cardiovascular benefit.
  • Supervised exercise therapy is first-line for claudication and rivals revascularization for walking distance.
  • Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor, improves claudication distance but is contraindicated in heart failure.
Question

What is the most important intervention for a smoker with claudication?

Smoking cessation, paired with a statin, antiplatelet therapy, and a supervised exercise program. For pure claudication, medical therapy and exercise come before any consideration of intervention.
