Questions you'll be asked

The rapid-fire teaching questions that come up on rounds and at the operating table, gathered in one place and grouped by the angle they come from. None of this is meant to intimidate. These are the questions a good teacher uses to find the edge of what you know, so rehearse the answers out loud and you will field them with composure.

Anatomy Physiology Clinical One-liners
How to use this chapter Cover the answer, attempt it aloud, then check yourself. The goal is not to recite trivia but to be able to reason in front of someone. "I am not certain, but here is how I would think about it" is a strong answer when you do not know, and far better than a confident guess on a service where guessing can harm a patient.

Anatomy

The questions you will most often be asked while scrubbed, because the anatomy is right in front of both of you.

Question

Name the branches of the abdominal aorta from top to bottom.

Celiac trunk (about T12), superior mesenteric artery (L1), the paired renal arteries (L1 to L2), the inferior mesenteric artery (L3), then the bifurcation into the common iliac arteries at about L4.

Question

Trace the arterial supply from the aorta to the dorsalis pedis.

Aorta to common iliac to external iliac to common femoral to superficial femoral (through the adductor canal) to popliteal to anterior tibial, which becomes the dorsalis pedis on the dorsum of the foot.

Question

What are the three tibial vessels, and which two reach the foot?

Anterior tibial, posterior tibial, and peroneal. The dorsalis pedis (from the anterior tibial) and the posterior tibial form the pedal arch; the peroneal ends above the ankle.

Question

What is the order of the aortic arch branches?

Right to left: the brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk, the left common carotid, and the left subclavian. The brachiocephalic then divides into the right common carotid and right subclavian.

Question

Which nerve travels in the adductor (Hunter's) canal with the femoral vessels?

The saphenous nerve, along with the nerve to vastus medialis. The superficial femoral artery becomes the popliteal at the distal end of the canal, the adductor hiatus.

Question

What is the main collateral around an occluded superficial femoral artery?

The profunda femoris (deep femoral artery). Its geniculate branches reconstitute the popliteal, which is why a healthy profunda can keep a limb viable despite an occluded SFA.

Question

Which nerves are at risk in a carotid endarterectomy?

Hypoglossal (tongue deviation toward the lesion), vagus and recurrent laryngeal (hoarseness), the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve (drooped mouth corner), and the superior laryngeal nerve (voice fatigue and pitch).

Question

Name the four compartments of the lower leg.

Anterior (deep peroneal nerve, anterior tibial vessels), lateral (superficial peroneal nerve), superficial posterior (gastrocnemius and soleus), and deep posterior (posterior tibial and peroneal vessels, tibial nerve).

Question

Where does the great saphenous vein join the deep system, and why does it matter?

At the saphenofemoral junction, into the common femoral vein. It matters because the great saphenous is the preferred conduit for both peripheral and coronary bypass, so it is mapped and protected.

Physiology & hemodynamics

Question

How does Poiseuille's law explain why a stenosis matters?

Flow scales with the fourth power of the radius, so a small reduction in lumen diameter causes a large drop in flow, and a slightly larger conduit greatly improves it.

Question

At what point does a stenosis become hemodynamically significant?

Roughly a 50 percent reduction in diameter (about 75 percent of cross-sectional area), where a measurable pressure and flow gradient appears across the lesion.

Question

Use Laplace's law to explain aneurysm rupture risk.

Wall tension is proportional to pressure times radius, so as an aneurysm enlarges, wall stress climbs at the same blood pressure, which both raises rupture risk and drives further expansion.

Question

How do you calculate and interpret an ABI?

Divide the higher ankle systolic pressure (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) by the higher brachial systolic pressure. Normal is 1.0 to 1.4, claudication typically 0.4 to 0.9, and critical ischemia below 0.4. Above 1.4 means non-compressible, calcified vessels, so use a toe pressure.

Question

Why can the ABI be falsely normal or high in a diabetic patient?

Medial calcinosis makes the vessels incompressible, so the cuff cannot occlude them and the pressure reads artificially high. The toe-brachial index is more reliable because the digital arteries are usually spared.

Question

What happens hemodynamically at aortic cross-clamp and unclamp?

Clamping raises afterload and stresses the heart. Unclamping drops afterload and washes out potassium and acid from ischemic tissue, causing declamping hypotension, which is why the clamp is released slowly with volume on board.

Question

How long can skeletal muscle tolerate ischemia?

About 4 to 6 hours before irreversible injury, which is the clock that makes acute limb ischemia an emergency and lowers the threshold for fasciotomy after revascularization.

Question

Why give a beta-blocker before a vasodilator in aortic dissection?

To reduce the shearing force on the wall. A vasodilator alone causes reflex tachycardia that increases shear and can propagate the dissection, so heart rate and contractility are controlled first.

Clinical & operative

Question

Embolic versus thrombotic acute limb ischemia, how do you tell them apart?

Embolic is sudden in a leg with no prior symptoms and normal contralateral pulses, often from atrial fibrillation, lodging at the common femoral bifurcation. Thrombotic is more gradual in a patient with known PAD and diseased pulses on both sides, with developed collaterals.

Question

A patient has an immediately threatened limb (Rutherford IIb). What do you do?

Anticoagulate with heparin at once, then revascularize urgently, because IIb is salvageable only with immediate restoration of flow. There is usually not time for a slow lytic infusion, so it goes for mechanical clearance or surgery.

Question

When do you repair an asymptomatic AAA?

At 5.5 cm in men, 5.0 cm in women, with rapid growth (over 0.5 cm in six months or 1 cm in a year), or if it becomes symptomatic at any size.

Question

How do you manage a hypotensive patient with a suspected ruptured AAA?

If unstable, straight to the operating room with no CT. Use permissive hypotension, keeping the systolic only high enough to perfuse the brain, because aggressive resuscitation can disrupt the contained rupture.

Question

Which carotid patients benefit most from intervention?

Symptomatic patients with 70 to 99 percent stenosis benefit clearly, ideally treated within about two weeks of the event. Symptomatic 50 to 69 percent is a moderate, individualized benefit. Asymptomatic high-grade disease is more selective.

Question

What is the best conduit for an infrainguinal bypass?

The patient's own great saphenous vein, especially for below-knee targets. Prosthetic grafts are acceptable for above-knee femoropopliteal bypass but do poorly distally.

Question

Which endoleak is most common, and which ones must be treated?

Type II (branch backfilling) is most common and is often observed. Types I and III are high-pressure leaks that re-pressurize the sac and must be treated.

Question

A heparinized patient's platelets fall on day 7 and a graft clots. What is going on?

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Stop all heparin, start a direct thrombin inhibitor such as argatroban, send the antibody and serotonin-release assay, and do not transfuse platelets.

Question

Bloody diarrhea on day two after open AAA repair. What do you suspect and how do you confirm?

Colonic (sigmoid) ischemia from interrupted inferior mesenteric and internal iliac flow. Confirm with bedside flexible sigmoidoscopy and treat early, since transmural necrosis is lethal.

Question

How do you image the arterial tree in a patient with a serious contrast allergy or poor kidneys?

Carbon dioxide angiography below the diaphragm, since CO2 is neither nephrotoxic nor allergenic. It must not be used in the cerebral or coronary circulation because of gas embolism risk.

Question

Why a self-expanding stent in the SFA rather than balloon-expandable?

The SFA flexes and is compressed with walking. A nitinol self-expanding stent springs back after deformation, whereas a balloon-expandable stent could be permanently crushed.

Question

Hypotension and back pain after a femoral puncture, with a normal-looking groin. What is it?

Retroperitoneal hemorrhage from a high stick above the inguinal ligament. Resuscitate, reverse anticoagulation, check a hemoglobin, and get a CT, because it is occult and can be fatal.

Classic one-liners

Trigger to answer, the kind of single-fact associations that come fast and are expected to be reflexive.

Reflexes worth owning
  • Most common source of an arterial embolus. The heart, usually atrial fibrillation.
  • Most common site an embolus lodges in the leg. The common femoral bifurcation.
  • Strongest modifiable risk factor for AAA and the single best PAD intervention. Smoking, and smoking cessation.
  • Screening for AAA. One-time ultrasound in men 65 to 75 who have ever smoked.
  • Amaurosis fugax. Transient monocular blindness from a retinal-artery embolus; the carotid is symptomatic.
  • Leriche syndrome. Buttock and thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction, and absent femoral pulses.
  • Pain out of proportion to exam. Acute mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise.
  • SMA embolus spares the proximal jejunum. Because it lodges distal to the first jejunal branches.
  • Chronic mesenteric ischemia triad. Postprandial pain, food fear, and weight loss.
  • Median arcuate ligament syndrome. Celiac compression by the diaphragm; treat by release, not a stent.
  • Phlegmasia cerulea dolens. Massive iliofemoral DVT with a swollen blue leg; a limb-threatening emergency.
  • May-Thurner syndrome. Left common iliac vein compressed by the right common iliac artery.
  • Paget-Schroetter. Effort thrombosis of the subclavian vein in a young, active arm.
  • Subclavian steal. Proximal subclavian stenosis with retrograde vertebral flow, more often on the left.
  • Most common peripheral aneurysm, and its danger. Popliteal; it thromboses and embolizes rather than ruptures, and is often bilateral.
  • Most common visceral artery aneurysm, with a pregnancy caveat. Splenic artery, which can rupture in pregnancy.
  • The Rule of 6s. A mature fistula: flow over 600, diameter over 6 mm, depth under 6 mm, 6 cm of vein, by 6 weeks.
  • Buerger's disease. Thromboangiitis obliterans in young male smokers; the cure is absolute cessation.
  • Venous ulcer location, arterial ulcer location. Medial malleolus versus the toes and pressure points.
  • Cornerstone treatment, venous versus arterial ulcer. Compression versus revascularization (and never compress an ischemic leg).
  • Protamine reverses heparin. Roughly 1 mg per 100 units; give it slowly because of the hypotension and anaphylaxis risk.
  • Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran; andexanet reverses the factor Xa inhibitors.
  • Intraoperative heparin before clamping. About 80 to 100 units per kg, with an activated clotting time above roughly 250.
  • The 6 Ps of acute limb ischemia. Pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia, with paresthesia and paralysis the ominous late signs.
