Open operations

The cases you will scrub. For each: the indication, the key steps in order, the anatomy you will be asked to name while holding a retractor, and the complications to watch for afterward. Read the relevant entry the night before, then re-read it once it is fresh.

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA)

Removal of the atherosclerotic plaque at the carotid bifurcation to prevent stroke. The patient is positioned with the neck extended and turned away, and the incision runs along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid.

» The steps, in order

  1. Expose the carotid sheath. Open it to find the common, internal, and external carotid arteries and the vagus nerve coursing within. Dissect with care, since manipulating the bulb can dislodge plaque.
  2. Heparinize, then clamp. Give systemic heparin and confirm the activated clotting time before clamping the internal, common, and external carotids in a deliberate order.
  3. Decide on a shunt. A temporary shunt can carry flow to the brain during the clamp, used routinely by some surgeons and selectively by others based on stump pressure or neuromonitoring.
  4. Arteriotomy and endarterectomy. Open the artery, develop the plane, and remove the plaque with a smooth distal end point so no flap is left to lift.
  5. Close, usually with a patch. A patch (bovine pericardium, Dacron, or vein) widens the lumen and lowers the restenosis rate compared with primary closure.
  6. Restore flow in sequence. Flush and unclamp in an order that directs any debris into the external carotid first, away from the brain, before opening the internal carotid.
Question

Which nerves are at risk during a CEA, and what does each injury look like?

The hypoglossal (CN XII), causing tongue deviation toward the side of injury. The vagus (CN X) and its recurrent laryngeal branch, causing hoarseness. The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve, causing a drooped corner of the mouth. And the superior laryngeal nerve, affecting voice pitch. The hypoglossal and vagus are the classic answers.

Red flag · the three early disasters

Neck hematoma can compress the airway fast, so an expanding neck swelling with stridor is a surgical airway emergency, and the wound may need to be opened at the bedside. Hyperperfusion syndrome (ipsilateral headache, seizures, hemorrhage) follows restoring high flow to a chronically low-flow hemisphere, and hinges on strict blood-pressure control. A new focal neurologic deficit on waking suggests an embolus or a technical flap and may mean going back to the operating room.

Open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair

Replacement of the aneurysmal aorta with a prosthetic (Dacron or PTFE) tube or bifurcated graft, through either a transperitoneal or a retroperitoneal approach. It remains the durable repair, used when anatomy is unsuitable for a stent graft or in younger, fit patients.

» The steps, in order

  1. Expose the aorta. Reach the neck of the aneurysm above and the iliac vessels below, identifying the renal arteries and the inferior mesenteric origin.
  2. Heparinize and clamp. Give heparin, then apply the proximal clamp (infrarenal where possible) and the distal clamps. The moment of clamping causes a sharp rise in afterload that stresses the heart.
  3. Open the sac and sew the graft. Open the aneurysm, control back-bleeding lumbar arteries from inside, and sew the graft to healthy aorta proximally and to the distal target.
  4. Reimplant vessels as needed. Reattach the inferior mesenteric artery or accessory renal arteries when colonic or renal perfusion depends on them.
  5. Unclamp carefully, then close the sac over the graft. Release the clamp gradually to blunt the drop in afterload and the washout of metabolites, then wrap the old aneurysm wall around the graft to separate it from the bowel.
Question

What happens hemodynamically at aortic cross-clamp and unclamp?

Clamping sharply raises afterload and can strain the left ventricle, so it is a high-risk moment for cardiac ischemia. Unclamping causes a fall in afterload plus washout of acid and potassium from ischemic tissue, producing hypotension ("declamping shock"), which is why the surgeon and anesthesiologist coordinate a slow release with volume loading.

Red flag · colonic ischemia

If the inferior mesenteric artery and the internal iliac supply are interrupted without adequate collateral flow, the left colon and sigmoid can become ischemic. The warning sign is bloody diarrhea in the first days after repair. Diagnose with bedside flexible sigmoidoscopy and treat early, because transmural necrosis is lethal. The leading cause of death after open AAA repair overall remains myocardial infarction.

EVAR & endoleaks

Endovascular aneurysm repair excludes the aneurysm from within by deploying a stent graft through the femoral arteries, so the blood now flows through the graft and the sac is meant to depressurize. It is less invasive than open repair, with lower early mortality but a need for lifelong surveillance imaging. The detailed mechanics live in the endovascular chapter; what every trainee must know cold are the endoleaks.

» Endoleaks, the defining complication

An endoleak is persistent blood flow into the aneurysm sac after stent-graft placement, which keeps the sac pressurized and at risk. The five types are a perennial question.

Question

Which endoleak is the most common, and which types must you treat?

Type II is the most common and is often watched if the sac is stable. Types I and III are high-pressure leaks that need treatment, because they re-pressurize the sac and carry a real rupture risk.

Lower extremity bypass

Rerouting blood around an occluded segment with a graft, the embodiment of inflow, outflow, and conduit. You choose a healthy inflow source above the disease, a target (outflow) vessel with good runoff below it, and a conduit to bridge them. Common configurations are femoropopliteal (above or below the knee) and femoro-tibial or femoro-pedal (distal) bypass for more extensive disease.

Pearl · conduit choice

The great saphenous vein is the best conduit and gives the best long-term patency, especially below the knee. Prosthetic grafts (PTFE or Dacron) work acceptably for above-knee femoropopliteal bypass but perform poorly for distal targets. This is why the team protects the saphenous vein and maps it before harvesting.

Question

Reversed vs in-situ vs non-reversed saphenous vein bypass, what is the difference?

All use the saphenous vein. Reversed vein is harvested and flipped so the valves do not obstruct flow. In-situ leaves the vein in its bed and the valves are cut with a valvulotome, which keeps the vein in its native size match (large proximally to the large inflow, small distally to the small target). Non-reversed translocated vein is removed and lysed of valves but reoriented. The in-situ technique's advantage is the favorable size match at each end.

Red flag · the early clotted graft

A bypass that loses its signal or pulse in the first hours to days has usually failed for a technical reason (a kink, a retained valve cusp, a clamp injury, or poor runoff). This is an emergency that warrants prompt return to the operating room or the angiography suite, not observation.

Hemodialysis access

Creating durable access for hemodialysis, almost always elective, and a large share of a vascular service's elective volume. The guiding principle is autogenous (fistula) before prosthetic (graft) before catheter, because a native arteriovenous fistula lasts longest and has the lowest infection rate.

Mnemonic · the Rule of 6s

A fistula is generally mature and usable when it meets the rule of 6s: a flow above 600 mL/min, a diameter above 6 mm, a depth less than 6 mm below the skin, and at least 6 cm of straight cannulation segment, by about 6 weeks.

Red flag · steal & ischemic monomelic neuropathy

An access can divert so much flow that the hand distal to it becomes ischemic, which is dialysis-associated steal syndrome (a cool, painful, pale hand, worse during dialysis). Severe cases need a flow-reducing procedure or ligation. A rarer, immediate post-operative ischemic monomelic neuropathy presents as sudden hand weakness and pain with preserved perfusion and is a surgical emergency.

Amputations

When tissue is nonviable or infection is uncontrollable, amputation is reconstructive, not a failure; it removes dead or septic tissue to save the patient and restore function. The level is chosen to balance healing potential (more proximal heals more reliably) against rehabilitation potential (more distal preserves function).

Question

Why try to preserve the knee with a below-knee amputation, and when is a guillotine amputation used?

Keeping the knee joint sharply lowers the energy cost of prosthetic walking and improves the chance a patient walks again, so a BKA is favored whenever it will heal. A guillotine (open) amputation is a quick, open, two-stage approach used to control active infection or wet gangrene; the wound is left open to clear sepsis, then a formal closed amputation is done later.

Embolectomy & thrombectomy

The classic operation for embolic acute limb ischemia. Through a cutdown on the common femoral artery (or the brachial artery for the arm), a Fogarty balloon catheter is passed beyond the clot, the balloon is inflated, and the catheter is withdrawn to drag the embolus out, repeated until inflow and backflow are brisk. A completion angiogram confirms the result.

Question

What instrument is used for a surgical embolectomy, and who invented it?

The Fogarty balloon catheter, developed by Thomas Fogarty. It transformed embolectomy from a major arteriotomy into a controlled extraction through a small cutdown.

TEVAR & complex aortic repair

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair places a stent graft in the descending thoracic aorta, used for thoracic aneurysms and for complicated type B dissections. When the aneurysm involves the visceral or renal branches, fenestrated and branched grafts (FEVAR and BEVAR) preserve flow to those vessels; the device side of these is covered in the endovascular chapter.

Red flag · spinal cord ischemia

Covering a long segment of the thoracic aorta can interrupt the intercostal arteries that feed the spinal cord, risking paraplegia. The protective measures are a lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drain to lower the pressure around the cord and deliberate maintenance of a higher mean arterial pressure to keep spinal perfusion up. Coverage of the left subclavian artery may compromise the arm, the posterior circulation, or a prior coronary bypass that used the internal mammary, so it is often revascularized.

Fasciotomy

Releasing the fascia to relieve compartment syndrome, the limb-threatening rise in pressure within a closed muscle compartment, classically after reperfusion of an ischemic leg, after trauma, or after a fracture. The diagnosis is clinical, with pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch as the earliest and most reliable signs.

Question

Name the four compartments of the lower leg and the structures most at risk.

The anterior (deep peroneal nerve, anterior tibial artery; injury causes foot drop), the lateral (superficial peroneal nerve), the superficial posterior (gastrocnemius and soleus), and the deep posterior (posterior tibial and peroneal vessels, tibial nerve). A standard leg fasciotomy uses two incisions to release all four.

Red flag

Do not wait for pulselessness, which is a very late finding. If compartment syndrome is suspected after a revascularization or trauma, the threshold to perform a four-compartment fasciotomy is deliberately low, because the cost of a delayed release is dead muscle and a lost limb.

Other operations to recognize

Aortobifemoral bypass
Aortoiliac occlusive disease

A bifurcated graft from the infrarenal aorta to both common femoral arteries, the durable open answer to Leriche-type aortoiliac disease with excellent long-term patency.

Fem-fem & axillofemoral
Extra-anatomic bypass

Routes that avoid a hostile abdomen. A femorofemoral crossover feeds one ischemic leg from the other side; an axillofemoral graft feeds the legs from the axillary artery in a patient unfit for an aortic operation.

Open thrombectomy of a graft
Salvaging a clotted bypass

Reopening a thrombosed graft, often combined with fixing the underlying cause (a stenosis at the anastomosis or in the runoff) so it does not simply clot again.

First-rib resection
Thoracic outlet

Decompression of the thoracic outlet for neurogenic or venous (Paget-Schroetter) syndromes, removing the first rib to free the neurovascular bundle.
